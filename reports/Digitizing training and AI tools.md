# Redraw for the placement, then let machines stitch

Professional digitizers turn a detailed logo into customer-ready embroidery by **redrawing it separately for each placement before a single stitch exists**. They do not convert the original. They scale the art to its final size, decide how much can be left out, fix the art in vector, rebuild the lettering from a matching font, and get the customer's approval on that altered art. Only then do they build objects back to front, picking each object's stitch by its width at final size, and sequence one pass per color. Erich Campbell's own files show what that means for one logo: **77,146 stitches and 17 colors on a full back, 17,380 on a left chest, and 7,201 stitches in 6 colors on a hat front that keeps only the mascot** ([Campbell, Takeup Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1849s)). Customers judge the result on whether the text reads at **3 to 4 ft "handshake distance"**, on clean, full coverage with no puckering, and on whether it matches the proof they approved. Simplification only causes complaints when it comes as a surprise. No AI service or model available today turns a building-plus-serif-wordmark logo into a production file unaided. Wilcom, the vendor with the most to gain from automation, says its own auto-digitizer is "not a substitute for expert digitizing" ([Wilcom](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/autodigitizing-concept)). The app's experience with its "Simplified" redraw sewing far better than the original is therefore the professional workflow showing through, not a fluke. The next gains come from making that redraw **placement-specific**, taking the **lettering out of the image model's hands** and rebuilding it from fonts, tracing the redraw **deterministically**, and wrapping the result in a **proof sheet** the customer approves. The tools worth integrating sit before and after stitch generation (vectorizers, an optional human-digitizer fallback), not in it.

## Nine steps from print logo to sew-out, and the redraw is the pivotal one

Every narrated professional walkthrough follows the same order. Campbell's big-project breakdown starts by **interviewing the client about what matters**: the blends in one client's jacket-back art turned out to be accidental ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=17m21s)). He then lists "obstacles and opportunities": more colors than needles, tiny details, text too small to run ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=18m56s)). His written checklist adds elements likely to distort, difficult gradients and thin outlines that need tight registration, and says to upscale or eliminate small text and hint at fine detail rather than render it "painstakingly" ([Campbell, Mr X Stitch](https://www.mrxstitch.com/digitizing-demystified)). The governing question for detailed emblems is not how to fit everything in but **"how much can I leave out"** while keeping the design's essence: "You want to draw trees. We don't want to draw leaves" ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=7m39s)). He recognizes a seal by its *parts*, such as a large eagle over a small eagle, a banner in the claw and a cactus. Once those parts read, beaks and feather counts are unnecessary ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h1m9s)). He works at real scale on a 1 mm sub-grid, because the common mistake is zooming in and forgetting the size of needle and thread ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h0m36s)). John Deer digitizes at a fixed 600% so that 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 mm can be judged by eye ([Embroidery Legacy live](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GllZBub-aEU&t=9m52s)).

The third step is where the pros and the auto-digitizers part ways. **Commercial shops "prepare all of our art pre-digitizing"** in vector software and settle layout and approval first, because hidden areas are never digitized and moving shapes later means redrawing ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=3m43s)). Campbell is explicit about how print vector differs from embroidery-ready vector. Print art has hidden stacked layers and shapes that merely touch. Embroidery art needs **shapes broken apart and overlapped** (touching shapes gap once stitched), no hidden duplicates, and flat ends on satin shapes ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=15m19s)). His raven example went from a print vector of "one gray slab plus outlines" to overlapped satins, carved talons, curved fills and satin borders on top, with all the black detail sewn as "one nonbroken filament" ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=1h23m11s)). The redraw for a small placement has fewer elements, thicker even strokes, opened negative space, enlarged or abbreviated text, and colors roughly halved. On one job, six blues became two threads with varying density giving three shades ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=17m53s)). The theater-logo redraw that finally worked had fewer scrolls and opened centers ([Ep. 193](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lvwB_ThN9tU&t=39m33s)). Campbell even names AI-generated logos as a typical problem input: gradients, lettering too small, too much detail, and a layout that doesn't fit the garment ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=16m10s)). He insists on **showing the customer the altered art first**, because redigitizing layered embroidery is far harder than editing vector ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1h4m18s)).

After approval the construction is mechanical in the good sense. Objects stack **from the back of the picture plane forward**, from large fills to detail, with satin details on top of fills and no gaps left in fills for the satins. Hats are the exception and run center-out ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=22m42s)). Each color runs once unless it truly must return ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=24m20s)). The file is replayed in the simulator, exported, reopened and replayed again to catch filtered stitches and jumps where travel runs belong. Then comes the rule that frames everything: **"your product is never the file"**, so sew it and judge the result ([Ep. 20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGPpUpJighw&t=56m33s)).

The only true step-by-step building walkthrough found is Sue Brown's lighthouse, which shows how a structure decomposes. She first traced it as outline primitives: a roof triangle, a lamp circle, a lamp-room rectangle, a thin gallery bar and stacked tower rectangles node-edited to fit a single tapered template ([Sue Brown, OML](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=348s)). The tapered silhouette is the element "your eye" goes to ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=1m48s)). Tower bands became separate fills ("you wouldn't want to do satin" on big pieces), the small lamp and roof became satin, and the outline became a run offset slightly inward as a shadow line ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=15m23s)). Texture was hinted, never filled. **Windows are too small for satin** and became run outlines retraced in one motion ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=38m17s)). A brick emboss fill is useless at small size ("you'll only get like one brick"), so she hand-placed a few run bricks and copied them around ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=42m46s)). Her optimizing pass then cut the design **from 22 colors to 7 with roughly the same ~10,000 stitches** by merging each color into one group joined by short runs hidden under later colors, putting the grass first and the black outline last so it covers the joins, and loosening an over-dense radial fill from 1.0 to 1.5 mm spacing ([Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=2103s); [Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=1263s)). She claims "90% of digitizers" skip this pass ([Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=44m22s)).

One gap matters for the app's test logo. **No narrated professional walkthrough of a university crest, church, skyline or detailed mascot exists on YouTube.** Searches returned music-only service montages. John Deer's own lighthouse breakdown is a paid $99.95 masterclass whose public outline confirms the same layered back-to-front plan ([Embroidery Legacy course page](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=379211)). The building guidance below is therefore assembled from the lighthouse, Campbell's seals and badges, and the Embroidery Legacy logos, not from a single worked example of the same kind of art.

## One logo, three files: the back keeps detail, the chest keeps the lockup, the cap keeps the icon

Professionals never scale one stitch file across placements. Campbell's screen shows the Buzzard's Roost logo as separate tabs, "Full Back", "Left Chest", "Hat Front" and "Hat back", each with its own stitch and color count ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1717s)). Ricoma demonstrated why on camera. A flat file run top-down and outside-in on a cap puckered at the bottom and sides and lost registration. The fix was a separate cap version sequenced bottom-up and center-out, with letters left of center running right to left ([Ricoma TV](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JJEl1TC66lE&t=3m14s)). Ricoma's advice is to get separate cap and flat files whenever sizes differ ([Ricoma TV](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JJEl1TC66lE&t=10m29s)).

| Placement | Typical size | What survives | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full back | about 9.5 × 8.5 in (Buzzard's Roost); up to a 15 in hoop | Everything wider than the satin minimum: panel seams, rigging lines, feather texture, outlined lettering. Large letters become tatami with a satin border, not wide satin. | 77,146 stitches, 17 colors ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1815s)); ~99,000 stitches hooped twice ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=31m20s)) |
| Left chest | 3.5 to 4.25 in wide | The full lockup and every color, but thin outlines and shadows removed, texture lines dropped, fills flattened, and text enlarged or moved so it reads at handshake distance | 17,380 stitches at 3 15/16 × 3 1/2 in ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1717s)); ASU polo width ([ASU](https://brandguide.asu.edu/execution-guidelines/apparel-merchandise)) |
| Cap front | about 2.25 in (≈60 mm) tall, 4 to 6 in wide | Usually **one icon**. Text moves to the back arch or side panels, long words are abbreviated, filler dots go, and the file runs bottom-up and center-out. | 7,201 stitches, 6 colors, mascot only ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1849s)); cap proportions ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=23m6s)) |

The left chest is where most of the app's customers will judge it, and Campbell's Sullivan Contracting case is the model. At 4 in wide or less on light pique, his analysis slide reads "Shadows are too thin to reliably run" and "Text can not run well *and* remain in this position." He offered two layouts: text kept small and balanced with a decorative line, or text moved and enlarged to read at handshake distance. **The customer chose the larger tagline**, and the cap got only the interlocked "SC" monogram ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=2412s)). On a fire-department badge his annotations read "Expand text to fill available area; Edit glyphs for clarity," "Reduce detail, open up tight spots," "Reduce gradients" and "Redraw arms" ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=2935s)). The cap logic is cropping. A tall full-body figure would have had a face "like a pencil eraser", so he cropped to head and shoulders, enlarged it and carved the hair in satins ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=23m6s)). A balloon logo became the balloon alone, with text in the empty wedges beside it ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=30m49s)). When a customer says another shop managed the full logo, he notes, that shop almost always **abbreviated, removed or changed the font** ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=1h7m34s)).

University brand offices reach the same answer by a different route: they swap in a simpler approved mark rather than redraw the detailed one. Arkansas says "stitching the logo by itself is often the most elegant option" and limits text under the logo to one line at 1/4 in ([UArk](https://brand.uark.edu/graphic-identity/embroidery.php)). Cal Poly lets the shield go on one side of the chest and the unit name on the other side or a sleeve, which lets the logo be bigger ([Cal Poly](https://ucm.calpoly.edu/merchandise-and-trademark-licensing/merchandise-guidelines)). ASU sets a 5 mm embroidery minimum height and says to use the vertical lockup, or screen-print, when the horizontal one cannot meet it ([ASU](https://brandguide.asu.edu/execution-guidelines/apparel-merchandise)). Buffalo sets 1/4 in cap height for added text ([UB](https://buffalo.edu/brand/TrademarksLicensing/resources/embroidery-guidelines.html)). Texas A&M forbids embroidered logos under 1.125 in tall ([TAMU](https://marcomm.tamu.edu/creative-platform/logos/logos-on-merchandise)). The University of Tennessee publishes **no embroidery rules**. It does require one of its licensed vendors and approval from its Office of Trademark Licensing, and it allows no more than one logo in a design space ([UTK designing merchandise](https://brand.utk.edu/promos-licensing/designing-merchandise/); [brand.utk.edu/logos](https://brand.utk.edu/logos/)). For a departmental mark like the microbiology logo, the customer may need to obtain an approved file or approval. The app cannot settle that, but it can say so.

Applied to the app's test logo, the sourced rules imply three different designs. The specific treatments in this table are **inferred** from the rules above, not taken from any source that handled this logo.

| Element | Full back (~10 in) | Left chest (~3.5–4 in wide) | Cap front (~2.25 in tall) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Building line art | Keep as two-pass run line work where lines stay ≥0.8 mm apart. Roof and wall planes as fills with neighboring angles differing. | Silhouette plus 3–6 large blocks (walls, roof, tower) as fill or satin; windows as a few run marks or dropped; no hatching | Silhouette and roofline only, possibly the building alone as the icon |
| Trees | 1–3 textured masses with trunk runs | 1–3 masses or a single shape per tree; never leaves | Drop, or merge into the building's base mass |
| TENNESSEE (serif) | Rebuilt from the closest serif font as satin columns | Satin from a matching font, enlarged; serifs simplified if cap height is small | Keep as the main text if it fits ≥5 mm, else move to the back arch |
| MICROBIOLOGY | Satin | Satin at ≥5–6 mm cap height, block caps | Back arch or side panel |
| EST. 1794, KNOXVILLE | Satin | Enlarge to ≥4–5 mm, or two-pass straight-stitch caps if it must stay small, or move or drop with the customer's approval | Drop, or back arch |

## Element by element: silhouettes, masses, satins and straight-stitch text

The detailed rules converge across Campbell, Embroidery Legacy, Digitizing Masterclass, Romero Threads and Impressions. What decides each element's stitch is its **width at final size**. Gaps too small for satin are outlined in straight stitch ([Ep. 52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JriWC4p0_zw&t=1h8m14s)). Logo outlines are satin at **1.6 to 2.0 mm**, detail satins run 1.6 to 3.2 mm, and the minimum satin is about 1 mm ([Embroidery Legacy wolf](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=agA2mYMuo7g&t=3m57s)). Letter strokes under 10 mm are usually satin ([Ep. 52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JriWC4p0_zw&t=18m19s)). Two courses closer than about 0.4 to 0.5 mm read as a solid fill, so doubled detail lines need at least 0.8 mm between them, and openings under 0.8 to 1.0 mm close in 40 wt thread ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/?p=169753)). The video frames show the same logic in an unexpected place. Embroidery Legacy built a ~70 mm mountain range **not as a fill but as point-counterpoint satin facets**, each angled along its facet, because each facet was narrow at final size ([Embroidery Legacy, Clear Skies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=1800s)). The width of the facet decides the stitch, not the size of the region.

Thin print lines are the most common trap in building and emblem art. On the Clear Skies badge, a divider measured on screen at 0.6 to 0.7 mm was judged too thin and **rebuilt as a 1.4 mm satin path** ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=1032s); [Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=2700s)). At small sizes, thin outlines turn to mush. A logo just under 3 in with every letter outlined "looked like mush" until it got a fill slab plus one contour border around the whole word ([Ep. 52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JriWC4p0_zw&t=58m10s)). Light-colored straight stitches look "broken and stitchy", so run outlines stay dark ([Ep. 193](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lvwB_ThN9tU&t=54m13s)). Corporate Casuals calls this the "dotted line effect" ([Corporate Casuals](https://www.corporatecasuals.com/articles/Digitizing_Custom_Embroidered_Designs_from_Graphics.asp)). Building detail survives as *lines between* things, not as the things. On a house the size of a dime, Campbell stitches the border, shadow and panel lines, never the wall panels themselves ([Ep. 52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JriWC4p0_zw&t=1h9m17s)). Mountains on a small badge are outline only ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h25m29s)). Shading collapses to 1 to 3 tones. Campbell offsets adjacent fill angles by about 10 to 12°, keeps shading layers at the *same* angle so they blend, and uses the lightest density that covers ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=34m8s)). Gradients from print are "a fool's errand" to copy ([ASI 2022](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/july-2022/how-to-turn-your-logos-into-embroidery/)). The one written-up landmark case, a lighthouse logo, had its gradient lines removed and wave highlights redone with two thread colors plus thread texture ([Corporate Casuals](https://www.corporatecasuals.com/articles/Digitizing_Custom_Embroidered_Designs_from_Graphics.asp)).

Small text has the firmest numbers of any element:

| Cap height | Treatment | Source |
|---|---|---|
| under ~3–4 mm | Open block capitals in straight stitch, **an even number of passes over every line**, dark on light. A tiny "A" crossbar is one stitch over and one back. Never satin at 2 mm. | [Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h3m17s); straight-stitch text under ~5 mm in all caps ([Ep. 33 frame](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=3797s)) |
| ~4–5 mm | Satin columns 1.2 mm (wovens) to 1.4 mm (knits), no underlay, counters ≥0.8 mm, uppercase; 60 wt with a 65/9 needle is crisper | [Digitizing Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7J1HqHr73Es&t=1m5s) |
| ≥5 mm | Satin, column ≥1 mm (ideally 1.2), density 0.40–0.50 mm, center-run underlay only, ≥1 mm inside letter holes, no overlaps between strokes, avoid lowercase | [Romero Threads](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdV0Mlx6JJo&t=0m0s) |
| Campbell's floor | 4–4.5 mm on coarse duck with a 75/11 needle and 40 wt; he refuses 1 mm text | [Ep. 255](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CkUqoBGq4gk&t=59m31s) |

Print-on-demand vendors are more conservative, at **0.25 in (about 6 mm) text and 0.05 in (about 1.3 mm) lines**, and Custom Ink says thinner lines "are converted to a single 'run stitch' or removed" ([Custom Ink](https://www.customink.com/help_center/embroidery-design-tips)). Banner text should use an envelope warp, not text-on-path, which leaves gaps and letters crashing into corners ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h2m45s)).

Serif wordmarks are **re-lettered, not traced**. For the Road Devils jacket back, Campbell identified the "idiosyncratic glyphs" found in no font, found the closest font, hand-drew only the missing R and D, removed photo distortion, evened out letter heights, warped in vector, got approval and only then digitized. One satin R took 5 to 6 objects ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=31m52s)). Font outlines themselves need rework: a B with an offset notch won't form a continuous satin, and auto-column on font outlines rarely works ([Ep. 243](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7-t5mSjs3xo&t=1h18m11s)). He regrets using keyboard text where a hand-drawn face didn't match ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=1h9m46s)). On camera, Embroidery Legacy rebuilt the CLEAR SKIES serif wordmark with a similar built-in satin serif font laid over the art, not an exact trace ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=1210s)). Beveled serif glyphs get separate facet objects with stitch angles opposed at about ±15°, serifs 45 to 90° off the column, splits at sharp turns, and sharp points extended because push narrows them ([Ep. 255](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CkUqoBGq4gk&t=9m44s)).

### What the frames showed that the narration did not

Pulling 101 frames from seven videos at decision moments turned spoken advice into visible settings. The numbers cluster tightly. **Runs are 3.0 mm**, switched to **bean ×3** when an outline must read bold ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=1300s); [Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=1633s)). Standard tatami is **3.5 mm long at 0.50 mm density** ([Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=386s)). Satin borders are **1.3 mm and 2 mm wide at 0.4 mm density** with sharp mitred corners ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=787s); [Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=847s)). Satin underlay is parallel at 2 mm spacing, 3.5 mm length and 0.5 mm inset, with center run off ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=2700s)). Decorative light-beam fills were loosened from 1.0 to 1.50 mm after being judged "way too dense" ([Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BmzhPbj2A7g&t=1263s)). The wolf mascot's hair spikes are tapered satins about **1.7 mm** wide, its travel is ~4.7 mm runs hidden under the later outline, and the red fill's boundary is drawn **through the middle of the black outline band** so red never shows outside it. The result was 6,918 stitches in 2 colors at 2.44 × 2.76 in ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=agA2mYMuo7g&t=237s); [Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=agA2mYMuo7g&t=1229s); [Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=agA2mYMuo7g&t=1366s)).

Three habits were visible but rarely stated. First, **small details are separate objects laid on top of base fills**, not holes cut into them: the lighthouse's anchor, star, windows and door each sit over the fills ([Sue Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Oz7VSQYnSA&t=2330s)). Second, inside a 2 in seal **tiny figures are just jagged straight-stitch strokes over a single fill**. At 1280% zoom a skull roundel is a handful of strokes, yet in 3D view it reads as a clean symbol ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=5096s)). Tiny stars are straight-stitch pentagrams joined by one travel line ([Ep. 33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHtAHrA3Zsc&t=4641s)). Third, when the logo color matches the garment, the pro adds a **contrasting backing fill sequenced first**. Clear Skies got white fills under everything, ending at 13,409 stitches and 69.4 × 76.4 mm ([Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=2590s)). The frames also expose what remains unknown. Pull-compensation panels were collapsed in every frame, fill angles in degrees were rarely visible, and no video showed the same building at chest and cap size side by side.

## Customers judge legibility at handshake distance and punish surprises

What makes a result "customer-ready" is consistent across shops, though no source gives complaint statistics or numeric tolerances. A cap supplier's list of eleven failure modes names the ones a digitizer controls: counters that fill in, distortion, excessive density, missing underlay (blamed on auto-tracing), **detail loss with text under 5 mm, blurred fine lines and muddy gradients**, and uneven density that leaves gaps or stiff "heavy patches" ([Promo Punks](https://promopunks.com/blogs/news/custom-embroidered-caps-in-australia-11-things-that-go-wrong)). Quality checklists add full, even coverage with no fabric showing, outlines that meet their fills, trimmed thread ends, and no puckering ([WeAllSew](https://weallsew.com/what-makes-a-good-embroidery-design/); [Stitchi QC checklist](https://www.stitchi.com/blog/embroidery-quality-control-checklist)). Steve Freeman calls small lettering "the No. 1 villain", says it fills in "like blobs", and puts the floor at 4 mm for a sans-serif ([ASI 2017](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/december-2017/thread-isnt-ink/)). Jeremy Picker's first check is whether the logo was made only for print, then line thickness, text size and counters ("Tiny, thin lettering doesn't work well in thread"). He uses a digitizer who sends physical sew-outs with every file ([ASI 2026](https://members.asicentral.com/news/strategy/february-2026/qa-jeremy-picker-discusses-the-challenges-and-rewards-of-translating-logos-into-embroidery/)).

The decisive variable is expectation, not fidelity. The legibility test is **3 to 4 ft "handshake distance"** ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=39m39s)), and embroidery is "meant to be seen at a distance" ([Corporate Casuals](https://www.corporatecasuals.com/articles/Digitizing_Custom_Embroidered_Designs_from_Graphics.asp)). Customers accept heavy simplification they have seen and approved; Sullivan Contracting chose the larger, moved tagline once shown both options ([Ep. 217](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6d9sQ09bKnE&t=2412s)). Freeman says that explaining why something won't work, and offering options, keeps customers happier and can increase sales ([ASI 2017](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/december-2017/thread-isnt-ink/)). The standard approval package is a placement mockup, a "digital sew-out preview", finished size, and thread names and codes. Production begins only after approval, and the customer is told to check small text and detailed areas because embroidery "may look slightly different" from print ([Lead Apparel](https://leadapparel.com/pages/artwork-review-and-proof-approvals)). One shop sends three versions side by side: the original as supplied, an impractical option and an embroidery-edited version (snippet only, [A&M Custom Clothing](https://support.amcustomclothing.co.uk/?p=797)). University licensors may require a physical sew-out on the ordered fabric before production or trademark approval ([UB](https://buffalo.edu/brand/TrademarksLicensing/resources/embroidery-guidelines.html)).

For the app, this reframes "customer would reject it" as two separable failures. One is a **stitch-quality failure**: closed counters, mushy outlines, gaps, density. Better geometry and rules fix that. The other is an **expectation failure**: the customer sees detail vanish without being told. A proof that shows the original beside the embroidery version, with a plain list of what changed and why, fixes that. Professionals handle both, and the second costs almost nothing to build.

## No AI produces a production file for this logo; the useful tools sit before and after stitching

The survey of auto-digitizing found one-click features in every desktop suite and a crop of browser "AI digitizers", but **no independent side-by-side stitch-out test of any of them**. The vendors' own words set the ceiling. Wilcom positions auto-digitizing for customer previews and price quotes, says designs "often need editing or full redigitizing for production-quality embroidery", and says it is "not a substitute for expert digitizing" ([Wilcom](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/autodigitizing-concept)). Practitioners list the same failures: small text "blurry or broken", gradients as messy blocks, one setting for all fabrics, too many stitches and jumps ([Hola Embroidery](https://holaembroidery.com/blog/general/can-ai-digitize-embroidery)). Those are exactly the hard parts of the Tennessee logo. Research code mostly produces *pictures* of embroidery, not stitch files. **No public dataset pairs artwork with professional stitch files**, which is why no end-to-end model exists.

### SVG-generating models and embroidery research code

The OpenAI Images API now accepts **`output_format: "svg"`** on generations and edits. An image model renders a reference, then a separate "author" model writes SVG markup reproducing it. This is a redrawing, not a trace. It requires `n=1` and "a supported image model and project access" ([OpenAI Images API](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/images.md)). Runware's guide confirms there is no seed, so results vary, and recommends prompting for flat fills, a short named palette and text converted to outlines ([Runware](https://runware.ai/docs/models/openai-gpt-image-2-6-flare/guides/svg-output.md)). The app reaches ChatGPT through the Codex CLI on the user's plan, not through an API key. Whether SVG output is reachable that way is **unverified**. It most likely needs a paid API key, which would break the app's no-key model. StarVector (Apache-2.0) beats VTracer on one benchmark at 8B parameters (DinoScore 0.966 vs 0.954), but its 1B model trails VTracer, and the authors say it "won't work on natural images or illustrations" ([StarVector](https://github.com/joanrod/star-vector)). OmniSVG needs 17 to 26 GB of VRAM and its training dataset is non-commercial ([OmniSVG](https://github.com/OmniSVG/OmniSVG)). Neither is realistic on a typical Windows PC. "Embroidery style" image generators make pictures of stitches. A UK supplier warns against prompting "embroidery style" for art meant to be digitized and recommends "flat vector style" on a clean background instead ([AM Custom Clothing](https://support.amcustomclothing.co.uk/index.php/knowledge-base/why-your-ai-generated-artwork-might-not-work-for-embroidery/)).

On the stitch side, the one useful research artifact is **Directionality-Aware Design of Embroidery Patterns** (CGF 2023). It traces fill stitches along a user-given direction field and writes Bernina `.emb`. Its code is **MPL-2.0**, an undocumented prototype that needs region and direction annotations ([GitHub](https://github.com/desmondlzy/embroidery-streamlines); [project](https://desmondlzy.github.io/publications/embroidery)). It is relevant to fills that follow a roof or facade but does nothing for satin text, underlay or compensation. MSEmbGAN and the diffusion-LoRA embroidery papers generate images only ([MSEmbGAN](https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/tg/5555/01/10643390/1ZAxnrnJd7O); [arXiv 2509.18948](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.18948)). EmbroideryForge chains rembg, vtracer, an LLM planner and pyembroidery much as the app does, but it has no licence file and no track record ([GitHub](https://github.com/EXPFAL/Embroideryforge)). Ink/Stitch is GPLv3, so its code should inform rather than be linked into a closed app ([Ink/Stitch](https://github.com/inkstitch/inkstitch)).

The pattern across this landscape is clear. AI is already good at the step professionals spend judgment on, **simplifying the art**, and the app has proven that with its Simplified redraw. AI is unreliable at the step that needs exactness, the **letterforms**. Deterministic code is better at turning clean art into geometry and stitches. The architecture that follows puts the image model in the redraw, fonts in the lettering, a tracer in the geometry, and the app's own engine in the stitches.

## Conclusion

The Simplified redraw sewing better than the original is the most important data point the app has. It shows that the bottleneck was never stitch generation but the artwork handed to it, which is exactly where professionals spend their judgment. The redraw is currently one generic recipe, though, and professionals make three different ones. They also never let the drawing step touch the lettering: they retype it in the nearest font and hand-fix only the odd glyphs. The app's remaining rejections on the Tennessee logo most plausibly come from those two gaps. First, a whole-lockup redraw at one size forces a building, two serif words and a tiny date line into a space that, at left chest, holds roughly a 17,000-stitch design and, on a cap, only an icon. Second, text that is traced from pixels or redrawn by an image model where it should be rebuilt as satin or straight-stitch type.

The second implication is commercial. Because customers punish surprises rather than simplification, a proof sheet showing the original beside the embroidery version, with plain notes on what changed, is worth as much as any stitch improvement. A paid "have a person check it" option at $15 to $30 covers the cases no automation reaches today. Keeping every human-corrected job, with consent, would start building the paired artwork-to-stitch dataset the whole field lacks.
