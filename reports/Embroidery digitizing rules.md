# Measure, classify, simplify: how digitizers decide stitches

Professional digitizers choose a stitch from the **shape of a region at its final sewn size**, not from its width alone. A satin goes only on a true *column*: two roughly parallel edges, a clear direction of travel, about **1.2 to 7 mm wide** on garments and caps. Anything blobby, holed or wider becomes tatami fill. Anything under about 1 mm becomes a single, double or bean (triple) run, or gets dropped. The settings follow the same logic. Density starts near **0.40 mm row spacing** for 40 wt thread, but narrow satins and small letters are sewn *more open*, while wide satins get slightly denser through a width-based auto-spacing table. Underlay climbs a ladder by width: none, then centre walk, then edge walk, then edge walk plus zigzag. Pull compensation is set per side by fabric, from **0.20 mm on stable wovens to 0.40 mm or more on fleece and knits**. Sequencing runs back to front and centre outward, and on caps from the bill up and from the centre seam outward. Pros finish each local element and its border together, even if that costs a colour change. Short connectors are hidden under later stitching, connectors over about **2 to 3 mm** are trimmed, and ties go only around trims and colour changes. For an automatic digitizer, the biggest gains come from three things. The first is a size-legality and simplification pass before any stitches are made. The second is a column test, so "satin everything under 7 mm" stops happening. The third is building satins from boundary "rails and rungs" with an explicit corner classifier, rather than from raw skeleton normals. Vendors publish their principles and a few defaults, but almost never their internal thresholds. Many numbers below are therefore well-supported rules of thumb, and the final section marks which rules are sourced and which are engineering inferences.

## Satin is a column test, not a width test

The sources agree on a working band for satin and disagree only at its edges. Erich Campbell describes satin as the stitch for narrow elements such as borders and text strokes, anything "less than about a centimeter across". He warns that long satins become "loose, loopy and snag on anything that will see wear", and that machines slow down above 12 mm ([Mr X Stitch, Campbell](https://mrxstitch.com/the-only-stitch/)). Wilcom puts the recommended maximum at **about 10 mm**, 12 mm in some situations, and up to 15 mm with snag risk, and tells users to **auto-split satins at about 7 mm** ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/embroidery-stitch-techniques)). On wearables the practical ceiling is lower. Embroidery Legacy's cheat sheet gives **1.5 mm as the satin "safe zone"**, roughly 1.5 to 5.7 mm for wearables and 7 to 12.1 mm only for non-wearables ([Embroidery Legacy cheat sheet](https://www.embroiderylegacy.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/ELCheatSheet-1.pdf)). A service guide gives the same picture: a minimum of 1.2 to 1.5 mm, a maximum of 7 to 8 mm and a "sweet spot" of 3.5 to 5 mm ([embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/satin-stitch-digitizing-that-actually-sews-clean-point-counterpoint-1-8-mm-rules-and-settings-that-stop-pinching)). The floor is where sources disagree. Melco says **1.2 mm** ([Melco](https://melco.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/202861315-Small-Lettering)), PI World 0.8 mm ([PI World](https://www.piworld.com/embroidery-digitizing-tips-on-creating-designs-with-letters/all)), and ASI argues a column cannot be narrower than the roughly 1 mm needle forming it ([ASI](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/may-2018/keep-your-small-letters-crisp/)). Ink/Stitch wants stroke-rendered satin wider than **1.5 mm** ([Ink/Stitch](https://inkstitch.org/docs/stitches/satin-column/)). Caps push the minimums up, not down. An Impressions cap-lettering article states flatly: "I don't digitize satin text smaller than 5mm, or with satin columns narrower than 1mm" ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/digitizing-lettering-for-caps/29138/)).

Width alone is not enough, and this is the core flaw in a rule like "under 1 mm run, 1 to 7 mm satin, over 7 mm tatami". Every documented system adds a shape test. Wilcom's own shape tools reserve Turning Satin for "narrow column shapes" and send everything with holes to "Tatami Fill with Holes" ([Wilcom docs](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/26/en/OnlineHelp/Automatic/automatic/Auto-digitize_individual_shapes.htm)). Wilcom also warns that satin on large areas is unstable and recommends tatami for "shapes needing a stable surface" ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/embroidery-stitch-techniques)). The Soft Sight auto-digitizing patents, the most detailed public description of an auto-digitizer, make the test explicit. They sample distance-transform values on the skeleton and route an object to satin only if it is **"thin, predominantly regular"**: low thickness *and* low standard deviation. Everything else becomes fill ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en); [US6397120B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6397120B1/en)). A 4 mm-average leaf with a fat middle and pointed ends fails that test and should be filled. Bernina's help adds that satin fill suits "narrow shapes of varying width", so moderate width variation is fine while the shape stays narrow ([Bernina help](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Digitizing/stitches/Create_fills_with_satin_stitch.htm)). The reverse mistake matters too. Fill in small letters causes puckering and buildup ([Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/posts/top-mistakes-157798745)). Campbell cautions that over-relying on fill looks flat ([Mr X Stitch](https://mrxstitch.com/the-only-stitch/)).

Below the satin floor, the run-stitch family takes over, and line art follows a strict template. Lindee Goodall's rule for redwork is "either a double pass or a bean stitch on every line of the image, no more and no less". She prefers bean for **better registration and less looping**, keeps double runs to short segments, and minimises trims because tie stitches show more on runs than on satin or fill ([LindeeG](https://lindeegembroidery.com/blog/item/the-truth-about-redwork-designs-2)). Satin under 1 mm causes thread breaks and should become a triple bean ([embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/machine-embroidery-line-designs-essential-stitches-and-techniques-for-perfect-outlines)). Campbell notes each run pass is about **0.4 mm thick, 0.8 mm for two**, so every extra crossing visibly thickens a line ([Mr X Stitch, Pathing](https://www.mrxstitch.com/pathing/)). Run stitch length has a hard floor of **0.5 mm**, below which the machine effectively stitches in one hole and breaks thread. **1 mm is safe**, and stitches longer than 5 to 7 mm loop and snag on wearables ([Embroidery Legacy cheat sheet](https://www.embroiderylegacy.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/ELCheatSheet-1.pdf)).

Outlines are where amateur output most often goes wrong at small sizes. Lauren Volker advises removing outlines entirely or partially where possible and letting stitch direction and fill carry the detail. She also advises making the remaining outlines thicker, never placing two satin lines side by side (they merge), and using fewer outlines on hats because they shift ([Impressions, Volker](https://www.impressionsmagazine.com/embroidery/design-digitizing/design-manipulation-embroidery/)). When a satin outline is used it should be **at least 1 mm wide**, and fill letters need a satin outline for edge definition ([Impressions caps](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/digitizing-lettering-for-caps/29138/)). Fill choice itself is simple. Tatami is the default for large and irregular areas (Wilcom's common settings are **0.38 mm spacing, 4 mm length, 0.25 offset**). Contour fills give a curved light-and-shade look. Motif fills ignore stitch angle and switch auto-underlay off ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/embroidery-stitch-techniques); [Wilcom docs, motif fills](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Decorative/motifs/motifs-7.htm)). The old Soft Sight system picked a fill angle by trying **16 scan angles and keeping the one with the fewest fragments**, which is a useful automatic rule ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en)).

## Density, underlay and compensation scale with width and fabric

The density baseline is solid. Standard garments run fill at **0.40 to 0.50 mm between rows**, patches at 0.3 mm or less, and lightweight fabrics or very large fills at 0.6 mm or more. Standard satin is about 0.4 mm ([StitchFast](https://stitchfast.co.uk/blog/pull-compensation-underlay-density-technical-guide)). On pique, 0.40 mm tatami is "a safe standard". 0.35 mm risks a stiff "bulletproof" patch and 0.45 mm can let fabric show ([embroideryhooping.com, Hatch pique](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/hatch-auto-fabric-demystified-build-a-custom-pique-profile-and-stop-re-setting-everything-every-time)). Units cause real confusion. Pulse and Tajima "points" are 0.1 mm, so density 4.0 means 0.40 mm ([Pulse Autograph guide](https://www.tajimadst.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Autograph2_UsersGuide-Jan-8.pdf)). One widely circulated fabric chart labels values of 3.5 to 5.0 as "lines per mm", which only makes sense as points ([Sassy Digitizing](https://sassydigitizing.com/knowledgebase/embroidery-density-guide)). Ink/Stitch's default fill spacing of 0.25 mm is far denser than commercial practice and should not be copied as a default ([Ink/Stitch fill_stitch.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/fill_stitch.py)).

Satin density, however, should **not** equal fill density, and this is the most common settings mistake. Wilcom explains that in very narrow columns "stitch density may be too high and needle penetrations damage the fabric", while wide columns may fail to cover. Its Auto Spacing therefore recalculates spacing from a width table wherever the column width changes. **75% auto spacing "generally produces high quality embroidery"**, and lower percentages are denser ([Wilcom docs](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Digitizing/stitches/stitches-8.htm)). Wilcom's worked example: if the table gives 0.4 mm for a 4 mm column, 90% gives **0.36 mm** ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/stitch-spacing-or-density)). Impressions states the principle directly: "the smaller the letters or the narrower the column, the lighter the density that should be used" ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). Wilcom's actual width-to-spacing table is published only as an image and was not retrieved, so any numeric ladder is a reconstruction. Thread weight shifts the baseline: one shop chart puts 60 wt satin at **0.35 mm** against about 0.40 mm for 40 wt ([Battle Born Clothing](https://battlebornclothing.com/blogs/custom-apparel-guides-screen-printing-embroidery-dtf-tips/embroidery-needle-size-thread-weight-stitch-density-guide)). Caps are contested. One service chart lowers cap density 10 to 15% ([Sassy Digitizing](https://sassydigitizing.com/blog/digitizing/best-embroidery-digitizing-settings-for-hats-caps)), while Wilcom's qualitative auto-fabric table makes structured cap backing "tighter" ([Wilcom auto fabrics](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Digitizing/properties/properties-9.htm)). A digitizer should keep cap density at the garment baseline unless testing says otherwise.

Underlay follows a width ladder, and the sources agree on its shape if not its exact rungs. For small columns, edge-walk underlay "has a propensity to pop out the sides of the column", so use a centre walk. Steve Freeman's blunt rule is to **never use edge walk on small letters** ([ASI](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/may-2018/keep-your-small-letters-crisp/)). Wilcom's lettering guideline, as reproduced by Vector Design, is **no underlay under 5 mm letter height, centre run at 6 to 10 mm, edge run above 10 mm**, with centre run suiting columns of about 2 to 3 mm ([Vector Design citing Wilcom](https://www.vectordesign.us/small-text-embroidery-minimum-letter-height)). By column width, service guides converge on centre run below about 2 to 2.5 mm and edge run plus zigzag at about 3 to 6 mm. They put centre walk stitch length at **1.5 to 2.0 mm**, edge-run inset at **0.3 to 0.5 mm** and zigzag underlay spacing at **about 2 mm** ([EmbroidAI](https://www.embroidai.com/blog/types-of-underlay-in-embroidery); [Sassy Digitizing](https://sassydigitizing.com/embroidery/your-design-needs-an-underlayment)). Ink/Stitch's satin defaults are similar: a contour underlay inset of 0.4 mm per side with 3 mm stitches, a centre walk with 2 repeats so it ends where it began, and zigzag underlay at 3 mm spacing ([Ink/Stitch satin_column.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/satin_column/satin_column.py)). For fills, the underlay runs **counter to the cover**. Ink/Stitch sets it at fill angle **+90° with 3× the top row spacing** (about 1.2 mm under a 0.4 mm top) at the same stitch length ([Ink/Stitch fill_stitch.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/fill_stitch.py)). Wilcom adds that the margin should grow until underlay never peeks outside the cover ([Wilcom tatami underlay](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-11.htm)). Pile and stretch fabrics need heavier underlay, but stacking underlays can make a stiff patch that deflects the needle ([StitchFast](https://stitchfast.co.uk/blog/pull-compensation-underlay-density-technical-guide); [embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/hatch-auto-fabric-demystified-build-a-custom-pique-profile-and-stop-re-setting-everything-every-time)).

Pull compensation is where an implementation can silently be off by half. Wilcom's guideline table is absolute: **drill/cotton 0.20 mm, T-shirt 0.35, fleece/jumper 0.40, lettering 0.2 to 0.3**, applied to *both* sides by default ([Wilcom pull compensation](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-17.htm)). A code base that spreads "0.2 to 0.4 mm total" across two sides compensates about half of what Wilcom recommends. Service bureaux go higher: twill 0.3 to 0.4, fleece and performance knits 0.6 to 0.8, pique and towel 0.8 to 1.0, and caps 0.4 to 0.5 mm ([Infinite Digitizing](https://www.infinitedigitizing.com/blog/push-pull-compensation/)). Those figures do not say whether they are per side. Another guide warns that needing more than 0.60 mm on standard fabric signals a stabilisation problem, not a compensation problem ([embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/stop-gaps-and-egg-circles-in-hatch-3-push-pull-compensation-that-actually-stitches-out-clean)). Wilcom stresses there is "no universal formula". Satin shows pull most, circles flatten into ovals, knits need more than wovens, and push makes long satin columns overrun their ends ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/push-and-pull-compensation)). Fills shrink too: a 50 mm fill can finish at 47 to 48 mm uncompensated ([StitchFast](https://stitchfast.co.uk/blog/pull-compensation-underlay-density-technical-guide)). Ink/Stitch supports compensation as both millimetres and a percentage of width, plus a separate push compensation that shortens column ends ([Ink/Stitch](https://inkstitch.org/docs/stitches/satin-column/)). No authoritative numeric push value was found.

## Corners, curves and junctions decide whether satins look clean

Wilcom's Smart Corners give the only published angle defaults. Corners sharper than **20° are capped**, those sharper than **45° are mitred**, and those sharper than **110° are lapped** (lap is exclusive with cap and mitre). A cap whose stitches would exceed a "cap max length" becomes a mitre ([Wilcom smart corners](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/quality/quality-11.htm)). The same page's own example (mitre under 75°, cap under 45°) differs from its stated defaults, so these are best read as ranges. Melco supplies the length limit and the small-element escape hatch. It says to avoid satin stitches longer than **6 to 7 mm** and to cap only smaller corners. Small elements that cannot taper before becoming narrower than a needle get a **"complete overlap"** instead of a tapered mitre, common on small lettering and twill tack-downs ([Melco Design Corners](https://melco-service.com/source1/Design_Corners.htm)). Impressions adds that borders of **about 1.5 mm or thinner** with obtuse angles can simply turn the corner. Acute points such as star tips need perpendicular stitch lines near the tip so the border does not bow. Auto underlay over-fills tapered mitres, so wide corners should end "a stitch or two away from the corner" ([Impressions corners](https://impressionsmagazine.com/?p=16307)). Bernina warns that sharp satin corners create hard spots that damage fabric and needles ([Bernina help](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Digitizing/quality/Controlling_corner_stitching.htm)). One guide says pointed ends should never taper to 0 mm and should keep **0.8 to 1.0 mm between wrap points** ([embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/satin-stitch-digitizing-that-actually-sews-clean-point-counterpoint-1-8-mm-rules-and-settings-that-stop-pinching)).

Curves need inner-edge shortening because the outer edge travels farther than the inner. Wilcom's stitch shortening triggers when inner spacing drops below a percentage of nominal. It uses **up to 5 consecutive short stitches** in a jagged pattern, each defined as a percentage of full length, with optional randomisation to avoid visible lines ([Wilcom stitch shortening](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/quality/quality-15.htm)). Ink/Stitch's implementation is concrete enough to reproduce. A rail point closer than **0.25 mm** to the previous point on the same rail is pulled toward its partner by an inset of **15%** (a cycling list, capped at 50%) ([Ink/Stitch satin_column.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/satin_column/satin_column.py)). Ink/Stitch's stitch placement also explains why skeleton-normal satins wobble and fan while rail-based satins do not. A satin is two rails plus "rungs" that fix direction, with **at least three rungs** recommended. Stitches go at the same normalised fraction along each rail between consecutive rungs, the count comes from the longer rail, and spacing is measured perpendicular to the previous stitch on both rails, using the larger value. **The outside of a curve therefore controls spacing**, and a step more than 5% off target is rescaled ([Ink/Stitch rails.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/satin_column/rails.py)).

Ink/Stitch's automatic rung placement is the most transferable single algorithm in the research. It divides the path into 101 buckets, adds each vertex's squared turning angle to its bucket, and smooths with a 9-tap kernel. It then places rungs at **local minima of this "corner energy"**, the calmest stretches, thins them to **at least 2 mm apart**, and keeps a rung only if it crosses each rail exactly once ([Ink/Stitch stroke_to_satin](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/utils/stroke_to_satin.py)). The Soft Sight patents cover what Ink/Stitch leaves manual: junctions and ends. They anchor skeleton end points to sharp contour tips (**under about 30°**) and match the angular gaps at a junction to nearby contour concavities. They rebuild the hidden boundaries so each stroke is sewn whole, least-squares-fit and smooth the "stroke normals", and **split columns at bends of about 45° or sharper** so stitches do not bunch on one side ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en)). For wide satins, Wilcom's Auto Split breaks long stitches with about a **4 mm split length and 0.25 offset**, and user-drawn split lines go roughly perpendicular to the stitch angle ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/embroidery-stitch-techniques); [Wilcom user-defined splits](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Decorative/patterns/patterns-14.htm)). Small-text sources add one more constraint. Run stitches at 90° to a narrow column bunch, and frequent angle changes hurt legibility, so angles should be smooth and consistent ([Impressions, Ceniceros](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/mastering-micro-lettering/16301/)).

## Sequencing trades efficiency for registration, and registration wins

The professional order is back to front, fill before border, centre outward. Campbell frames it as the "tablecloth" rule: start near the centre and smooth fabric toward the edges. Never stitch *toward* an area already secured to the stabiliser, because that traps fabric into ripples ([Mr X Stitch, Pathing](https://www.mrxstitch.com/pathing/)). He wants each colour as one continuous thread where possible, but not at any price. When registration is at risk, he stays within one area rather than travelling across the design. Fine outlines over fills should sew immediately after their fill, because spreading them out raises loss of registration ([Mr X Stitch, Digitizing Demystified](https://www.mrxstitch.com/digitizing-demystified/)). Melco makes this explicit for caps: sew from the bill toward the crown and from the centre seam outward, and **finish elements as you go**, a letter or two and then its border. Melco calls this less efficient but tighter. It also warns that sewing toward an anchor point bunches material, and a stiff bill can deflect the needle. Its cap safe height is the distance from the bill to where the crown curves, **minus 1 inch**. Organic designs can go about 0.5 in over it ([Melco caps](https://melco-service.com/source1/Digitizing_for_Caps.htm)). Sequencing also manages push and pull. A column running the same direction as the fill beneath it sinks in, while one at a different angle stands up ([Impressions push-pull](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/anticipate-push-pull-in-designs/16265/)). This is the strongest sourced argument for varying angles between neighbours.

Connector thresholds cluster tightly. Bernina's Wilcom-engine software adds implicit trims and tie-ins when a connector exceeds **2 mm** ([Bernina help](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/es/MainHelp/PART_Digitizing/connectors/Connectors.htm)). Wilcom says connectors shorter than **about 3 mm** are usually invisible, but should be shorter when thread contrasts with fabric ([Wilcom trim settings](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/connectors/connectors-8.htm)). Ink/Stitch's auto-route trims any jump over **1 mm**, counting only the part of the jump outside both shapes ([Ink/Stitch satin tools](https://inkstitch.org/docs/satin-tools/); [auto_satin.py](https://git.push-f.com/inkstitch/plain/lib/stitches/auto_satin.py?h=nix)). Machine-side trimming is coarser: Brother machines trim jumps only from **5 to 50 mm** ([Brother PR670E manual](https://www.manualowl.com/m/Brother%20International/PR670E/Manual/606152?page=186)), so files should carry explicit trim commands. Trims are expensive. Wilcom estimates **about 70 stitches of run time per trim** ([Wilcom Help](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/embroidery-fonts-printing-fonts)). Its answer is Closest Join to align exits with entries, Branching to give touching objects one entry and one exit, and "Inside" connectors for letters within one lettering object ([Wilcom minimizing trims](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/minimizing-thread-trims-in-embroidery); [Wilcom closest join](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/closest-join)). Bernina's help sets the non-negotiable condition for travel runs: **connectors not covered by later elements must be trimmed** ([Bernina connectors](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Digitizing/connectors/connectors-1.htm)). Ink/Stitch's auto-route enforces it neatly. A satin segment traversed twice is sewn as a running stitch the first time, because it will be covered, and as satin the last time ([auto_satin.py](https://git.push-f.com/inkstitch/plain/lib/stitches/auto_satin.py?h=nix)).

Tie stitches are conditional, not universal. Wilcom's tie-in options are Off, Always, After Trim/Colour Change, or Previous Connector Longer Than a length. Tie-ins sit **inside the shape on the second stitch** and are "generally" used after a trim ([Wilcom tie-in](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/connectors/connectors-6.htm)). Tie-offs mirror this and come in three methods. Method 1 puts small stitches between the last two lines and suits dense fills. Method 2 goes up and back along the last line, looks better on small objects and satins, but risks breaks. Method 3 splits the second-last line to hide the tie. Critically, **narrow shapes and small lettering get only one tie-off stitch, optionally shortened** ([Wilcom tie-off](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/connectors/connectors-7.htm)). Tie-off distance should be no larger than trim distance ([Wilcom connectors](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/connectors-the-functions-between-objects)). Ink/Stitch likewise locks only when the next jump exceeds the minimum jump length, or around trims and colour changes, and it exempts lock stitches from minimum-length filtering ([Ink/Stitch lock stitches](https://inkstitch.org/docs/stitches/lock-stitches/); [Ink/Stitch preferences](https://inkstitch.org/docs/preferences/)). Tying at every object start and end therefore adds lumps on small details and wastes time without a sourced benefit. No source gives default tie length or count.

Layering completes the picture. Wilcom's Remove Overlaps cuts the hidden lower layer using the upper object as cutter. It keeps a **cutting overlap** to prevent gaps, excludes cutters narrower than a minimum width (borders and details), excludes light background fills, and discards fragments below a minimum size ([Wilcom remove overlaps](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/quality/quality-2.htm)). Wilcom publishes no defaults for any of these. Narrow strokes need no added overlap because pull compensation supplies it ([Wilcom custom fonts](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Management/custom_fonts/custom_fonts-23.htm)). Campbell warns that cookie-cutter removal under small overlying elements creates aligned edges with extreme density ([Impressions, Campbell](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). That is a reason to apply knockout only for large cutters. A service guide gives adjacent-fill overlap of **0.2 to 0.4 mm**, with zero-overlap butt joints acceptable only on rigid caps with heavy buckram ([embroideryhooping.com](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/embird-digitizing-that-cute-clip-art-gift-box-without-the-bulk-clean-polka-dots-true-negative-space-and-smoother-fills)). Knits need more overlap than nylon or twill ([Impressions corners](https://impressionsmagazine.com/?p=16307)).

## Small text and fine detail fail first, so simplify before stitching

Lettering floors are the best-documented numbers in the field. Hatch's per-font table puts block sans at **6 mm (0.25 in) minimum cap height**, serif at 6 mm, a run-stitch block font at **4 to 6 mm**, comic styles at 10 mm and script at **12 mm**. Lowercase sits at **about 70% of cap height**, so it falls below the floor first ([Vector Design summarising Hatch](https://www.vectordesign.us/small-text-embroidery-minimum-letter-height); [Hatch blog](https://blog.hatchembroidery.com/choosing-the-right-size-embroidery-lettering-for-your-project/)). Melco gives 0.25 in for hats and 0.20 in for flat goods. It does not recommend serif fonts at small scales and says artwork **details smaller than 2 mm need modification** ([Melco guidelines](https://melco.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/10706046938381-Embroidery-Design-Guidelines); [Melco small lettering](https://melco.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/202861315-Small-Lettering)). ASI allows **4 mm** if small text must stay, with thinner thread and needle and all caps ([ASI 2022](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/july-2022/how-to-turn-your-logos-into-embroidery/)). It also says left-chest and hat designs should not carry web addresses or phone numbers ([ASI 2017](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/may-2017/5-rules-for-better-fonts/)). Campbell has digitized micro-lettering down to 3 mm by tuning density, underlay and compensation ([Impressions Expo](https://texas.impressionsexpo.com/speakers/erich-campbell/)). That is the skilled-human ceiling, not an automatic default. Serif faces need more room, around **8 to 9 mm**, because their hairlines drop below 1 mm long before the letter does ([Logos Workwear](https://logosworkwearschoolwear.com/blog/choosing-fonts-for-embroidery); [Impress Designs](https://support.impressdesigns.com/hc/en-us/articles/20137093770644-What-is-the-minimum-font-text-size-for-sewing-embroidered-garments)). Wilcom notes that run-stitch fonts are the usual choice below 5 mm ([Wilcom Help](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/embroidery-fonts-printing-fonts)).

How small letters are sewn follows from those floors. They get light density. Service guides open spacing to **0.45 to 0.50 mm** for micro lettering ([Sassy Digitizing](https://sassydigitizing.com/blog/embroidery/small-lettering-and-monogram-digitizing)) and 0.42 to 0.45 mm for quarter-inch text ([hoopingstation](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/crisp-0-25-lettering-in-melco-designshop-v11-the-settings-that-stop-dimples-bubbles-and-mushy-text)). Ceniceros says "less is always better in tight areas" ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/mastering-micro-lettering/16301/)). Underlay is minimal (centre run or none), and every source forbids edge walk. Some pull compensation is essential, since lettering without it "almost always looks poor" ([Vector Design citing ASI](https://www.vectordesign.us/small-text-embroidery-minimum-letter-height)), but values range from 0.1 to 0.4 mm with no consensus. Run-stitch text under about 4 mm uses **1 to 1.5 mm stitches** to avoid gaps ([absolutedigitizing](https://absolutedigitizing.com/how-to-digitize-small-letters-for-embroidery-without-losing-quality/)). Counters such as the eye of an "e" must be **at least 1 mm** ([Embroidery Legacy](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=240720)), because surrounding stitches pull inward and shrink openings. Amann adds that a 3 mm letter can need about 50 stitches and that letters should be connected rather than trimmed. It also says to slant stitches to keep small openings from closing and to remove or enlarge details that cannot be sewn ([Amann](https://www.amann.com/knowledgehub/blog/how-to-create-small-embroidery-designs-with-the-right-thread/)). Wilcom notes that scaling stock lettering changes connectors, and it trims inter-letter gaps over a set limit (example: 2 mm) ([Wilcom Help](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/embroidery-fonts-printing-fonts)). The industry consensus is to re-set text with fonts designed for the target size rather than trace it, and to shrink a stock font no more than 10 to 15% ([Tex-Inc](https://tex-inc.com/blogs/digitizing-embroidery-blog/digitizing-small-text-cleanly-stock-lettering-techniques-that-actually-work)). No public source confirms that any commercial auto-digitizer runs OCR.

Line art and illustrations follow a "render the impression, not the lines" rule. ASI says digitizers convey the design's overall sense, not every line, dot or colour shift ([ASI 2022](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/july-2022/how-to-turn-your-logos-into-embroidery/)). Campbell removes "small elements that won't stitch cleanly" and "overly-dense detail", hints at details rather than rendering them, and enlarges small elements and gaps so they stitch and stay open ([Mr X Stitch](https://www.mrxstitch.com/digitizing-demystified/); [Mr X Stitch, Prep and Punch](https://www.mrxstitch.com/prep-and-punch-drawing-and-embroidery-digitizing/)). Jesse Elliott's tree recipes are directly encodable. Leafy trees become random tatami clumps, mostly vertical (random about 80%), or a few simplified clumps sharing one direction. Pines become near-vertical random fill. Bushes get a jagged edge of about 0.6 mm at 45 to 90°. Grass is a fill plus a few satin blades, "never every blade" ([Impressions, Elliott](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/digitizing-realistic-designs-part-2-trees-and-bushes/16286/)). Embroidery Legacy reduces foliage to dark, medium and light texture areas rather than individual leaves ([Embroidery Legacy trees](https://embroiderylegacy.com/embroidery-digitizing-trees/)). For small logos, a simplified version made for embroidery (name plus primary symbol on a hat) beats shrinking the full print logo ([Pulsemerch](https://pulsemerch.com/embroidery-for-small-logos-guide-to-sharp-branding-apparel/)). Auto-digitizers fail in predictable ways. They treat small text and thin lines like large shapes, produce over-noded jagged outlines from raster art, and struggle with gradients and shadows. The professional fix is to rebuild borders and small text manually ([digitizingusa](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/why-auto-digitizing-fails)). A logo of the kind in the test case shows all of this: a building drawing, large serif "TENNESSEE", spaced caps and a small date line, at 4 × 2.25 in on a cap. The small date line almost certainly falls below every 4 to 5 mm floor and should be enlarged, converted to run lettering, or dropped. The building should become a bold silhouette with a handful of interior bean lines rather than a mesh of satin outlines.

## The documented algorithms: distance transforms, rail-based satins and Eulerian fills

The public algorithmic record is thin but coherent. Vendors such as Wilcom say only that their instant auto-digitize "chooses the most suitable stitch types" ([Wilcom docs](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Automatic/automatic/automatic-9.htm)). Hatch classifies colours as "fills or details" and renders details as centerline, satin line or satin fill ([Hatch help](https://hatch.embroideryhelp.net/v3/en/OnlineHelp/Automatic/auto-digitize/Auto-digitize_embroidery.htm)). The Soft Sight patents fill in the pipeline. They start from 300 dpi colour input, apply edge-preserving smoothing (high-contrast pixels untouched) and grow regions from low-contrast seeds. Leftover edge and noise pixels go to the best-matching neighbour, and objects **under 6 pixels are merged** into the nearest large neighbour. A (3,4) chamfer distance transform sampled on skeleton pixels then gives the maximum, mean and standard deviation of thickness for the thin/thick decision ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en); [US6397120B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6397120B1/en)). In Python, skan turns a skeleton into a per-branch table with path length and the mean and standard deviation of any value image. Passing the distance transform gives width statistics per branch, which allows a region to be part satin and part fill ([skan](https://skeleton-analysis.org/stable/getting_started/getting_started.html)).

Skeletons are unstable, and the fixes are known. Small boundary bumps create large spurs ([Discrete skeleton evolution](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_skeleton_evolution)). Bai, Latecki and Liu prune by partitioning the contour with discrete curve evolution, so every kept branch ends on a visually significant contour point ([Latecki et al.](https://cis.temple.edu/~latecki/Papers/OptimalSkeletonScienceChina2013.pdf)). Junction misreading is a leading reason artists distrust vectorizers. Frame-field methods resolve X and T junctions that a single tangent cannot represent ([Bessmeltsev & Solomon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01922)). Before skeletonising, vector cleanup matters. vtracer (MIT) offers a speckle filter, a **60° corner threshold**, a **45° splice threshold**, spline fitting, and a "cutout" mode where neighbouring shapes share boundaries instead of stacking ([vtracer](https://github.com/visioncortex/vtracer)). Potrace cannot trace centerlines ([Launchpad](https://bugs.launchpad.net/inkscape/+bug/170537/comments/5)). AutoTrace can, through thinning ([AutoTrace](https://github.com/autotrace/autotrace)).

Fill generation has a clean open reference. Ink/Stitch intersects the shape with parallel rows at the fill angle and treats rows and outline pieces as graph edges, doubling every other outline edge so an Eulerian path exists. It eulerises the graph if needed and walks it while **preferring row edges**, which avoids odd jumps between distant rows. Travel uses a lattice at ±45° (2 mm spacing) with edges weighted 3 × length ÷ (distance to outline + 0.1), so it hugs the shape's middle. Lattice edges are removed once a row covers them, so hidden travel is not reused. Holes smaller than row spacing squared are dropped, and outlines are simplified at 0.5 mm ([auto_fill.py](https://git.push-f.com/inkstitch/plain/lib/stitches/auto_fill.py?h=nix); [Inkscape-devel](https://lists.inkscape.org/hyperkitty/list/inkscape-devel@lists.inkscape.org/message/N5PPL6I2XAFMOKWTLCBVKYIBTV2L54K6)). Tatami rows stagger needle points; Ink/Stitch defaults to a **4-row stagger cycle** ([Ink/Stitch fill_stitch.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/fill_stitch.py)). Ink/Stitch is GPL, so its code should inform a re-implementation, not be copied. The Soft Sight patent US6397120 is listed as expired.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of an auto-digitizer. Stitch generation is the easy part. The decisive steps happen before it and after it. Before: a size-legality and simplification pass at final physical size that removes, merges or enlarges what cannot sew, and re-sets text instead of tracing it. After: sequencing that protects registration, with local finishing, covered travel and conditional ties. Within stitch generation, the leverage is in classification (per-branch width *and* uniformity) and in building satins from boundary rails with a corner classifier, not in tuning density by hundredths of a millimetre.

The remaining uncertainty is real. No vendor publishes its width-to-spacing table, its auto-digitize thresholds, push compensation amounts, overlap defaults or tie lengths. Several widely quoted figures come from service-bureau blogs that disagree with one another, notably cap density, towel pull compensation and small-text compensation. The rules below should therefore be exposed as tunable profiles (fabric, thread weight, cap vs flat) and checked against sew-outs, starting with the cap test logo.
