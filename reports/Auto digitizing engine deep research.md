# Sew more satin, cut fewer threads

Our own files give the clearest answer. We measured 66 finished stitch files that were really sewn for customers and compared them with what our digitizer produces. Three gaps stood out. Our fills laid **half the thread** (rows 0.46 mm apart, against about 0.2 mm). Our designs were **32% satin against 61%**, because we sewed bold lines as stacked running stitch. And our left-chest logo had **16.5 trims per 1,000 stitches against 3**. The first two were changed in the engine this round and now need a sew-out. Trims are the biggest gap left. The deeper research on six fronts points the same way: what remains is mostly about shapes and route, not settings. The engine must build each satin from its two edges with planned corners and joins, shrink the building by fixed millimetre rules before any stitch exists, keep letters connected, and check its own files the way a teacher would. One warning covers all of it. No source anywhere shows a professional digitizing a building logo, so the building rules are our own translation of other fields and must be proven on fabric.

A few words are used throughout. Each is explained once here.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Satin | A zig-zag column. The thread goes side to side across a shape. Used for letters, borders and bold lines. |
| Fill | Rows of stitches laid back and forth to cover a wide area. |
| Run | A single line of stitches, like a pen line. Bean is a run where each stitch is sewn three times. |
| Underlay | Light stitches sewn first, under the visible ones, to hold the fabric. |
| Pull and push | Thread pulls a shape narrower along the stitch and pushes it a little longer at its open ends. "Compensation" means drawing the shape to allow for that. |
| Trim | The machine stops, cuts the thread, moves and starts again. A tie is the tiny knot before and after. |
| Travel | Stitches that only move the needle to the next part. They should end up hidden. |
| Junction | A place where lines or letter strokes meet, like the middle of a T. |
| Registration | Whether parts land where they should, for example a border on the edge of its fill. |

## Our 66 production files: mostly satin, tight fills, three trims per thousand

The files came from a folder of real customer work on the project's own computer: left-chest and cap logos, name drops and patches. They were measured with the project's own tool, and only summary numbers were kept. Three limits apply. The files hold only stitches, so each stitch was sorted into satin, fill, run or bean from its shape. Files larger than about 16 KB could only be read for the first 5,300 stitches or so, which makes totals too low but leaves rates and widths sound. And the notes do not record the fabric or who digitized each file. With that said, this is the only evidence in the whole study that comes from finished production work, and it is treated as primary (our own measurements, `research_notes/Auto digitizing engine deep research/production_files_measured.md`).

| Measure (middle value, with the middle half of files in brackets) | Production files | Our output before this round |
|---|---|---|
| Trims per 1,000 stitches | 3.0 (1.7 to 5.4) | 8.0 overall; 16.5 on the left-chest logo |
| Share of thread in satin | 61% (32 to 78%) | 32% |
| Share of thread in fill | 18% (7 to 47%) | 33% |
| Share of thread in run | 13% (9 to 18%) | 20% |
| Share in bean | 1% (1 to 2%) | common: every bold line was four passes of run |
| Narrowest satin in a design | 1.3 mm (1.0 to 1.8) | 1.0 mm in lettering only; lines under 1.6 mm were run |
| Typical satin width | 2.2 mm (1.5 to 2.9) | 2.0 mm |
| Widest satin | 4.3 mm (2.9 to 6.8) | 3.8 mm |
| Satin spacing (advance per zig-zag pair) | 0.40 mm (0.36 to 0.42) | 0.40 mm |
| Gap between neighbouring rows of fill thread | 0.19 to 0.22 mm | 0.46 mm |
| Stitches per square inch of the design's box | 526 (261 to 794) | 511 |

**Our fills were half density, and the cause was a unit trap.** Digitizing software states fill "spacing" of 0.40 to 0.45 mm from one forward row to the next forward row. A return row lies between them. So the thread lines of a solid fill sit about 0.2 mm apart, and that is what the production files measure. We sewed rows a full spacing apart. Four outside sources point the same way. Erich Campbell teaches measuring fill density from one row, skipping the next, to the third ([Mr X Stitch](https://www.mrxstitch.com/4-basic-digitizing-skills/)). A university course calls 5 lines per mm "bullet proof" and 4 its working compromise ([EduTechWiki](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Stitch_Era_-_adjusting_stitch_sections)). A density-checking patent assumes four 40-weight threads fill 1 mm ([US 6,732,008](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6732008B2/en)). And a trade article counts 939 stitches in one square inch at default density ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/how-to-work-with-push-pull-compensation/16206/)), which by our arithmetic fits rows about 0.2 mm apart and is about double what 0.4 mm rows would give. The engine now sews fill rows at half the stated spacing. This is not "adding density" in the sense professionals warn against. Satin stays at 0.40 mm per pair, which already matched. In one repair video, satin set to 0.2 mm was named as a cause of a ruined logo ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/pHEsUIwDuyU?t=319)).

**Production work is mostly satin, and satin starts at about 1 mm.** Bean stitch is almost absent from the files. We had been sewing every line between 0.8 and 1.6 mm wide as four stacked passes of running stitch. The engine now sews a web of bold outlines as narrow satin along its cleaned-up centre lines, as one continuous thread: a run goes out along each line as underlay, and the satin comes back over it. The file mix matters here. Name drops and patches are satin-heavy by nature, so 61% is a direction, not a target for a line-art building.

**Trims are the biggest gap left.** Production files carry about 3 trims per 1,000 stitches. Our left-chest logo carried 16.5. Letters inside a word are not trimmed in the production files. Two more things follow. First, real files are not trim-free: at 3 per 1,000, a 6,000-stitch logo still has about 18 trims. So the goal is a rate inside the production band, not zero. Second, the overall stitch count per square inch was nearly the same in both sets (526 against 511). A stitch-count check alone would have passed our files while the thread was in the wrong places. Our on-screen preview also hid the thin fills, because it drew thread 0.42 mm wide. It now draws 0.32 mm.

## Professional engines build satin like a ladder and plan the route first

Patents from Soft Sight, Wilcom, Melco, Brother and Pulse, one university thesis, and the public Ink/Stitch documentation describe one shared design. Almost all the patents have expired. The engine finds the centre line of each shape and its width. Thin, even shapes become satin. Wide ones become fill. A satin is stored as a ladder: two long edges ("rails") and cross-lines ("rungs") that fix the stitch angle, with at least three rungs per column ([Ink/Stitch](https://inkstitch.org/docs/stitches/satin-column/); [US6397120](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6397120)). The rails should be pieces of the true outline, and each rung should end on the nearest edge points on either side of the centre line, which also stops rungs from crossing ([uOttawa thesis](https://ruor.uottawa.ca/bitstreams/1dd9cbf6-9804-4eae-9490-23393f3aa740/download)). Two rules from this material are for the builders, not for the stitches. Ink/Stitch and PEmbroider are open-source under licences that forbid copying their code into our product, so only their documented ideas were used. And one recent patent on reducing jumps (CreateMe, 2022) is still active and should not be copied as written ([US11725319](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11725319)).

**Junctions are where automatic engines fail, and the thesis says how.** A centre line grows small false branches at corners and square ends. The thesis removes a branch when it is no longer than **1.2 to 1.4 times** the half-width of the shape at the fork, and removes the twin branches at a square end with a factor of 2. At a fork it joins the two arms that run most nearly straight through and cuts the third arm off along a line between the two inside corners. It then lists its own errors: at four-way crossings the wrong arms get joined, T shapes break, and star shapes get over-cut. The author blames using only the direction right at the fork ([uOttawa thesis](https://ruor.uottawa.ca/bitstreams/1dd9cbf6-9804-4eae-9490-23393f3aa740/download)). The Soft Sight patent takes the opposite approach. It lists every possible pairing of arms, rebuilds the hidden edge through the junction for each, and ranks them ([US6397120](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6397120)). Putting the two together is our own inference: score each pairing by how straight the arms run over two or three stroke widths, and by how well their widths match. A related patent ends a column where it bends sharper than about 45 degrees and starts a new one, which is why a letter N becomes one diagonal and two uprights ([US8219238](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8219238)).

**Corners have published angles. Overlaps do not.** Wilcom's help gives the only numeric corner rules found. A corner sharper than 20 degrees gets a cap (a small extra piece over the tip). Sharper than 45 degrees gets a mitre (two pieces meeting on a diagonal). Sharper than 110 degrees gets a lap (one leg runs through, the other starts on top). A cap that would need too long a stitch turns into a mitre ([Wilcom](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/quality/quality-11.htm)). Erich Campbell adds a size rule: caps for small text, laps for medium to large, mitres for medium to very large, because mitres pile needle holes on tiny text ([The Takeup 266](https://youtu.be/z9mkSFmAH1U?t=1242)). No source publishes how much the pieces should overlap. Width limits are better covered. Melco switches from satin to fill at 6 mm ([US9702070](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9702070)), a Wilcom patent names about 12 mm ([WO2005061774A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2005061774A1/en)), and our production files top out at 4.3 mm in a typical design. Three independent lines of evidence agree that everyday satin lives between about 1 and 7 mm.

**Fills and routes are planned so travel ends up covered.** For fills, Wilcom shifts the needle points of each row by a quarter of a stitch length so no ridge lines form ([Wilcom](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Decorative/patterns/patterns-3.htm)). Soft Sight tests 16 fill angles and keeps the one that breaks the shape into the fewest pieces ([US6397120](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6397120)). Wilcom prefers travel that is short, stays away from the edge and crosses the later rows at a wide angle ([WO2005061774A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2005061774A1/en)). For whole groups, Melco's patent describes the trick our outline web now uses: treat the centre lines as a network, walk every line twice in one unbroken tour, sew the first pass as hidden underlay and the second as the visible stitch ([US9702070](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9702070)). Brother's patents add two ordering rules. Start and end a shape within 2 to 5 mm of each other by laying a hidden run to the far end and sewing back over it. And at a fork, finish the side branch before sewing the fork itself, so thread never crosses finished work ([US5560306](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5560306); [US5191536](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5191536)). Wilcom's own release notes list what goes wrong in its branching engine: travel showing on top of the cover, gaps where satins meet, some letters sewing wrong ([Wilcom release notes](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/es/OnlineHelp/Release/rn-27-notes/rn-27-notes-21.htm)). That list is a ready-made set of checks for ours.

## A building shrinks by rules: 0.8 mm between lines, 2 mm per detail

Nobody demonstrates a building. Our own search, about 55 queries on YouTube, found no narrated video of a professional digitizing a house, church, skyline or landmark logo. So the rules here come from two other places: map-making, which has solved "keep a detailed plan readable when it shrinks" for decades, and line-art videos on other subjects. Map-makers key every rule to one or two minimum sizes. On a printed map, parallel lines need about 0.25 mm between them and the smallest dot is about 0.3 mm ([Ledermann 2023](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/6/134/2023/ica-abs-6-134-2023.pdf)). Hollow shapes need nearly twice the size of solid ones: 1.0 mm against 0.6 mm in one classic table ([Ledermann 2022](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/139892/1/Ledermann%20Florian%20-%202022%20-%20Empirical%20assessment%20of%20minimum%20dimensions%20for...pdf)). For thread we swap in our two numbers: **0.8 mm** between lines and **2 mm** for the smallest detail. The 0.8 mm figure has trade support. Campbell writes that lines closer than about 0.5 mm read as a solid patch and doubled lines need 0.8 mm or more ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). The 2 mm figure and the whole translation are our inference. The same reviewer notes that even the map numbers grew from practice, not from rigorous tests.

**The map rules are concrete enough to code.** Monika Sester's building simplifier removes every wall edge shorter than a set length, shortest first, using three cases. A small bump or notch is flattened into the main wall. A small step is removed by extending the longer wall. A clipped corner is squared off. A part that is too small but important is kept by enlarging it, not deleted ([Sester 2000](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIII/congress/part4/931_XXXIII-part4.pdf)). For lines that run side by side, a study of how people read sketches gives three measured thresholds: lines count as "one line" when their directions are within **20 degrees**, the pair is at least **8.5 times** longer than it is wide, and a gap of **2.1 times** the usual spacing splits a group ([StrokeAggregator](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2018/StrokeAggregator/StrokeAggregator_authorversion.pdf)). Those thresholds came from two small studies of 15 people each. For walls, a geometry library straightens lines that are within a set angle of the main directions, 25 degrees by default ([CGAL](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Shape_regularization/index.html)). We suggest 10 degrees so roof slopes survive. For rows of windows, map-makers first make all the windows identical and then replace many with fewer, evenly spaced ([Guercke and Sester 2015](https://kartographie.geo.tu-dresden.de/downloads/ica-gen/workshop2015/genemr2015_submission_4.pdf)). Our own arithmetic turns that into a limit: with 2 mm windows and 0.8 mm gaps, a 55 mm front holds at most 19 windows, a 20 mm wing 6, and a 10 mm tower 3. A window that must show anything inside it needs to be about 4 mm across, from the hollow-shape rule.

**Texture goes. Trees become shapes.** No published method detects brickwork, hatching or foliage in a drawing and chooses what to sew instead. The test we propose follows from spacing alone: any patch whose lines sit closer than 0.8 mm cannot be sewn as lines, so it becomes a solid shape, a plain outline, or nothing. Practitioners say the same in words. Embroidery Legacy's artwork lesson removes textures, shadows and gradients, and says a detail that only reads when zoomed in will not survive ([Embroidery Legacy](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=199377)). Campbell says not to draw individual leaves on a tree at left-chest or cap size ([The Takeup 49](https://youtu.be/ldYCC-m3v5k?t=1977)).

**The line-art videos back the engine's new satin lines.** For a 3.7 inch outline portrait, John Deer sewed every visible line as satin 1 mm wide and the frame at 3 mm, with run stitch used only as hidden travel ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/--9VshtAmSk?t=91)). Lashley Creations drew every line of a bold drawing as satin and aimed for zero trims ([Lashley Creations](https://youtu.be/SMZPw6PhTyo?t=94)). Where two lines cross, she sews the front line unbroken and stops the back line either side, because two full satins stacked are too dense ([Lashley Creations](https://youtu.be/SMZPw6PhTyo?t=612)). Three more points matter for a building. Tracing each window pane as its own outline sews every shared bar twice, a fault Campbell shows in auto-digitized files ([The Takeup 49](https://youtu.be/ldYCC-m3v5k?t=3301)). A thin run outline around fills on a knit would not register in one repair video and was removed ([fiercekittenz](https://youtu.be/yW51-Bu0aWc?t=230)). And very detailed line art should not be sewn small at all ([Lashley Creations](https://youtu.be/Eex77rRhi3o?t=92)). Our building is about 55 mm wide, which is under the 3 to 4 inches that presenters call normal for a left chest ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/Pr_Du4rbxDE?t=45)). The simplifier should aim for the low end of every count.

## Forty-five walk-throughs and the free classes fix shapes and order, not settings

Forty-six videos were read as full transcripts, and 45 had usable content. Most come from Embroidery Legacy, Erich Campbell, Lashley Creations, Melco and Romero Threads. The most useful finding is what the presenters do not do. They rarely touch density, underlay or compensation, because a fabric preset sets them. The values they leave alone cluster tightly: 0.4 mm spacing, 2.5 mm run, satin split above 7 mm, no trim for moves under 2 mm ([Wilcom](https://youtu.be/Oy1rRVoFmeU?t=557); [Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/WH-RlNIHKOs?t=685)). Their time goes to three things: the shape of each object, the order, and where objects connect.

**Every defect shown in a sew-out had a shape or order fix.** A gap between a fill and its border came from the border only touching the fill. The fix was to overlap the border about half its width, shown with three sew-outs at 0, 50 and 100% ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/bhju-sCb8sg?t=45)). Letters "dancing" above and below the line came from open stroke ends drawn exactly on the art. The fix was to cut open ends short ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/pHEsUIwDuyU?t=409)). Letters splitting open came from two stitch directions meeting with nothing underneath. The fix was a small patch of run stitches under the junction ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/WH-RlNIHKOs?t=731)). Visible threads between letters came from joining at the very bottom of the strokes. The fix was to join one stitch up, so the connector is buried ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/2cBdv3nFlwA?t=319)). A gap beside a small shape came from a hole cut in the fill beneath it. The fix was to run the fill straight through ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/SwQgkSONGsg?t=1070)). Adding density was never the fix, and twice it was the cause.

**Trims are treated as a cost to design out.** One presenter prices a trim at about 150 stitches of machine time and a colour change at 175 ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/SwQgkSONGsg?t=660)). Melco counts five tie stitches per trim, which dimple small letters ([Melco](https://youtu.be/hovdha29VOg?t=459)). Three sources sew all small text with no trims between letters ([Melco](https://youtu.be/hovdha29VOg?t=368); [Extreme Wizards](https://youtu.be/PA9srZ5Grt8?t=513); [Romero Threads](https://youtu.be/03yLwBMflCQ?t=5676)). The same presenter found that an automatic "fewer colour changes" tool cut colour blocks from 16 to 6 but raised trims from 40 to 51 and saved no time ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/SwQgkSONGsg?t=1344)). Sources disagree on how long a connector may be before it must be trimmed: 2 mm is the software default, 3.5 mm was judged too visible in script lettering ([Embroidery Legacy](https://youtu.be/XONHrbr4lWI?t=273)), and one class tolerates 4 to 7 mm ([Wilcom class](https://youtu.be/x-YBP4-XoWo?t=994)). The answer depends on whether the connector is hidden.

**Small lettering has the firmest numbers.** With normal 40-weight thread, letters need to be about 5 mm tall, strokes at least 1 mm wide, and the holes inside letters at least 0.8 to 1 mm ([Campbell webinar](https://youtu.be/tZMuH5GfV7g?t=215)). Density sits at 0.40 to 0.45 mm. Underlay is a centre run only, because edge underlay pokes out of tiny columns ([Campbell webinar](https://youtu.be/tZMuH5GfV7g?t=1935)). Thinner 60-weight thread with a smaller needle allows letters about 25% smaller ([Campbell webinar](https://youtu.be/tZMuH5GfV7g?t=1485)). Below that, text is sewn as running stitch with exactly two passes on every stroke ([Campbell webinar](https://youtu.be/tZMuH5GfV7g?t=2862)).

**The free classes agree on the basics and give us a marking scheme.** The free material that carries real numbers is small: Embroidery Legacy's cheat sheets, Campbell's articles, the Ink/Stitch tutorials, a University of Geneva course and the vendor manuals. They agree on 0.4 mm density, a hard 12.1 mm longest stitch, light fill underlay at right angles to the top stitching, and letter openings of about 1 mm ([Embroidery Legacy cheat sheet](https://embroiderylegacy.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Digitizing-101-Cheat-Sheet-2025.pdf); [Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). They disagree on pull compensation (0.2 to 0.4 mm, or 5 to 15% of width), on the width where underlay type changes, and on the widest comfortable satin (5 to 7 mm). Teachers grade a file the same way everywhere. They replay it slowly and look for extra trims, doubled parts and missing ties. They measure letter openings, underlay position and clusters of needle holes. Then they compare a sew-out with the screen ([Impressions](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/isolating-embroidery-problems-digitizing-vs-execution-pull-push-distortion/168424/)). Every one of those checks can be run by the engine on its own output. The classes also teach a first step we can copy: before digitizing, give the artwork one of three verdicts, "as it is", "with changes" or "needs a redesign" ([Embroidery Legacy](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=199377)).

## A sewn fill shrinks up to 4.6%, and no preview shows it

Nobody has published a formula that predicts pull from stitch length, density and fabric. What exists is a small set of real measurements from Kaunas University of Technology. A 50 mm filled circle sewn on plain woven fabric came out as an oval, shorter along the stitch direction by **0.7 to 4.6%** of its radius ([Radavičienė 2014](https://epubl.ktu.edu/object/elaba:3344252/3344252.pdf)). Satin columns 3 to 7 mm wide sewed **12 to 25% narrower** than drawn, and the fabric under a 7 mm column buckled into a wave about 1.5 mm high, against 0.1 mm under a 3 mm column ([Radavičienė 2012](https://epubl.ktu.edu/object/elaba:3243049/3243049.pdf)). Those tests mention no backing or underlay, so they show the worst case. A later test on a backed knit with underlay and 0.2 mm of compensation still did not match the drawn size ([Daukantienė 2020](https://epubl.ktu.edu/object/elaba:40495065/40495065.pdf)). Commercial software does not model any of this. It adds a fixed allowance per side from a fabric table: 0.20 mm for cotton drill, 0.35 mm for T-shirt knit, 0.40 mm for fleece, 0.2 to 0.3 mm for lettering ([Wilcom](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-17.htm)). By our arithmetic, on a 4 mm column those allowances come to 10 to 20%, the same order as the lab figures. No measured values exist for pique, jersey, fleece or caps.

**The fill-then-border gap has been measured.** In the same circle test, a fill sewn first shrank, and the border sewn afterwards landed at its programmed place. The gap was **0.4 to 0.9 mm**, which the authors call visible to the eye ([Radavičienė 2014](https://epubl.ktu.edu/object/elaba:3344252/3344252.pdf)). That is the lab version of the maple-leaf sew-outs above, and it explains why a 1 mm border centred on the edge of a fill fails on its worst side. The overlap also cannot be the same all round. Edges where stitches end pull in. Edges that run alongside the stitches push out.

**Density and thread-break risk can be mapped from the file.** One expired patent lays a grid of 0.25 mm cells over the design, adds 2 for each needle hole and 1 for each thread passing over, and calls 6 or more "high". It suggests a design is fine when under 10% of its area scores above 5 ([US 6,732,008](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6732008B2/en)). Melco's manual gives the best-sourced limits on short stitches. Needles are 0.7 to 0.8 mm thick, so stitches should be at least 1.0 mm in satin, 1.5 mm in run and 2.0 mm in fill ([Melco](https://melco-service.com/source1/Stitches_Too_Small.htm)). No source publishes a trims-per-thousand benchmark. Our own 3.0 is the only one we have.

**Every preview found is pretty, not honest.** Published and open-source previews draw each stitch as a shiny ribbon in sewing order. None shrinks the shapes, sinks the thread or lets fabric show through. Ink/Stitch draws thread about 0.37 mm wide ([Ink/Stitch renderer](https://git.push-f.com/inkstitch/plain/lib/svg/rendering.py?h=nix)). Ours was wider still until this round, and it hid our half-density fills. An honest preview would apply the measured shrink to each part before drawing the next, and would never widen thread to close a gap. That design is our inference. The lab papers also hand us a cheap calibration test: sew a 50 mm filled circle and satin bars 3, 4, 6 and 7 mm wide on each real fabric, wait a day, scan at 600 dpi, and read off the shrink. A handful of sew-outs would turn lab numbers into numbers for the shop's own polos and caps. No public data set pairs stitch files with photos of their sew-outs, so nobody can train a model to do this for us. The nearest thing, a 2026 set of 1,080 photos of good and faulty embroidery, has no stitch files attached ([Vina-Embroidery](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/sp99tbxchp/1)).

## Bold letters stay satin if strokes are found, widths checked and joins tucked

Vendors are frank that converting a customer's typeface is second best. Hatch says not every font suits embroidery and advises a test stitch ([Hatch](https://help.hatchembroidery.com/portal/en/kb/hatch/tech-talk/articles/what-should-i-know-about-hatch-fonts-truetype-fonts-and-third-party-fonts)). mySewnet recommends satin conversion only for letters 12 to 30 mm tall and says medium-weight plain fonts convert best ([mySewnet](https://softwarehelp.mysewnet.com/MSM/120/Embroidery/Topics/Guidelines_for_Using_mySewnet™_QuickFont.htm)). A bold wordmark and small serif text are both outside that comfort zone, which is exactly why they need care. Wilcom's converter shows what any such tool must decide: the stitch angle at stroke ends, whether serifs are separate pieces (only when wider than **0.8 mm**), whether sharp bends are split or capped, how corners are detected, and how much strokes overlap at T joins and at the closing seam of round letters ([Wilcom](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Management/custom_fonts/custom_fonts-5.htm)). Wilcom publishes the options but not the values.

**The best published method for finding strokes fails on the boldest letters.** StrokeStyles, a 2022 research method, splits a letter outline into overlapping strokes without any template. It uses the centre line inside the letter, the inward dents of the outline, and cut lines between pairs of dents, and it names seven kinds of junction. It is written in Python and takes about 2 seconds per letter. On Chinese characters with known answers it scored 97.9% per pixel. It has no score for Western fonts, because no answer key exists. Its own stated main failure is very thick letters, where a stroke is wider than it is long ([StrokeStyles](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/31944/1/strokestyles-opt.pdf)). That is our bold wordmark. A simpler idea from font rendering helps: pair up opposite stretches of outline, call mutual pairs stems and one-way pairs serifs. As published it only handles upright and level strokes ([FreeType](https://freetype.org/autohinting/features.html)). Our inference is to run both, test up front whether the letter is too thick to trust the first, and check the result by laying the found strokes back over the letter. The research paper counts a stroke as wrong when it covers less than 80% of what it should.

**Width, not letter height, decides the stitch.** By our arithmetic, a black-weight letter with strokes about 0.3 of its height has 2.4 mm strokes at 8 mm tall and 6 mm strokes at 20 mm. Both are inside the plain satin range. Split satin is only needed above about 23 mm tall. No professional source describes black-weight letters at this size, so this is derived, not observed. The real risk at this weight is the holes inside letters closing, because wide columns pull in more.

**Hand-built fonts show how joins and serifs should be made.** Font builders keep columns between about 1 to 1.5 mm and 7 mm. Where two strokes meet, the preferred join keeps both strokes' stitches square and tucks one stroke under the other. A stroke that narrows to a point against another causes knots and broken needles and is to be avoided ([Embrilliance](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Win%201169/digitizingtipsforfontcreation.htm)). For a T, the upright sews first and the bar starts where it ends ([Embrilliance](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Mac%201170/satincolumnjoininglevel2.htm)). Under wide joins, a small satin "underlay bar" stops a visible split ([mySewnet](https://softwarehelp.mysewnet.com/MSW/120/FontDigitizing/Topics/Overlaps_and_Underlay_Bars.htm)). In small satin fonts the serif is part of the stroke end, not a separate piece ([mySewnet](https://softwarehelp.mysewnet.com/MSW/120/FontDigitizing/Topics/Serifs.htm)). Wilcom's own font list shows the limits in practice: its ordinary serif fonts are rated for 8 to 15 mm, its purpose-built small fonts for 4 to 6 mm, and its run-stitch fonts for 5 to 12 mm ([Wilcom font list](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/downloads/FontInclusions.pdf)). No source gives the overlap at a join in millimetres.

## Conclusion

The most useful result of this round did not come from reading. One afternoon of measuring the shop's own finished files settled a question that two earlier rounds of research had left open: what "0.40 mm" really means in a fill. The same method should now be turned on sew-outs. A circle, four satin bars and the test logo, sewn on a real polo and a real cap and scanned, would replace most of the borrowed numbers in this report with the shop's own. The engine should also print three rates on every export, next to the production band: trims per 1,000 stitches, share of satin, and the gap between fill rows. Those three would have caught every major fault found here. Total stitch count would have caught none.

The second change is in where effort goes. Settings are close to right, and professionals barely touch them. What separates a usable file from ours is the route (letters joined, travel hidden, trims at the production rate) and the shapes (ends cut short, strokes tucked under each other, borders lapped over fills, the building reduced before stitching). The largest unknown is the building itself. Every rule for it is translated from maps, sketches and portraits, because no professional has shown one. The test logo's next sew-out is therefore not a formality. It is the experiment.

## What could not be found

- **A building.** No narrated video or article shows a professional digitizing a building, house, skyline or landmark logo. No source gives a rule for windows, bricks, roofs or battlements in thread.
- **A pull formula.** No published formula predicts pull from stitch length, density, fabric and backing. No measured values exist for pique, jersey, fleece or caps. Push at a satin end has trade figures only.
- **Overlap sizes.** No manual publishes the overlap at mitre, cap or lap corners, at T joins, or between letter strokes. Wilcom shows the settings without their values.
- **How the big engines choose a route.** Nothing public explains how Wilcom or Pulse pick branch order or joining points, or how satin is split beyond "stagger the splits".
- **A trim benchmark.** No shop, vendor or paper publishes trims per 1,000 stitches or any score for how production-friendly a file is. Our own figure is the only one.
- **An honest preview.** No vendor documents how its 3D preview is drawn, and none was found that shows shrinkage or fabric showing through.
- **Paired data.** No public collection pairs stitch files with photos of their sew-outs.
- **A marking scheme.** No published rubric for grading a digitized file was found. The settings lessons and planning checklists of Hatch Academy and Embroidery Legacy's paid courses are behind a login.
- **Bold letters.** No professional source describes how black-weight letters 8 to 20 mm tall are digitized. The stroke-finding research has no accuracy figure for Western fonts.
- **Unsettled disagreements.** No sew-out compares bean with a two-pass run. The smallest satin width (1.0 mm against 0.7 mm) and the longest untrimmed connector (2, 3.5 or up to 7 mm) are not settled by any test.
- **Papers and manuals not read.** Several could not be opened or were too large: the full direction-field fill paper (Liu 2023), Sester's fuller 2005 paper, two line-drawing papers, Brother PE-Design manuals, and any official Pulse or Tajima documentation. The numbers in one lab paper are only in its charts.
- **Limits of our own measurements.** The 66 files carry no record of fabric or digitizer, larger files were only partly readable, and stitch types were worked out from stitch shapes. The notes record no sew-out yet of the two fixes made this round.
