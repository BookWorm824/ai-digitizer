# Rebuild the art before generating stitches

Professional digitizers do not convert a logo. They **redraw it at its final sewn size** as a small set of embroidery objects. Each object's stitch type comes from its measured width and its role in the picture, and stitches are generated only after that. Erich Campbell gives the clearest working bands: **running stitch for lines under 0.8 mm, satin from 0.8 to 10 mm (auto-split above about 7 mm on wearables), and fill above 10 mm**. Parallel lines stay **at least 0.8 mm apart**, and lines closer than 0.5 mm are merged, because they sew as a solid. The settings sit under a few hard ceilings. Top density is never tighter than **0.40 mm** with 40 wt thread. Thin coverage is fixed with perpendicular underlay at **3 to 4 mm spacing**, not with more density. Pull compensation is roughly **0.2 to 0.35 mm** per side and push compensation **0.13 to 0.20 mm** at column ends. Fill angles sit **15 to 20° off horizontal, never 45°**. Sequencing aims for **one continuous thread per colour**, with travel hidden under later stitching and trims only where registration or layering requires them. Every symptom in the current output on the university logo traces back to the root cause professionals blame on auto-digitizers: the software traces pixel edges and colour blocks instead of building objects. **Wobbly walls** come from fitting noisy contours with many nodes instead of a few straight segments snapped to the building's axes. **Blob trees** come from flood-filling line art that should have been thinned to at least 0.8 mm line spacing, or rendered as textured, partial-density masses. **Ragged serif fills** come from putting tatami on narrow, serifed strokes. Professionals build those as satin columns with the serifs split off, edge-run underlay underneath and a satin outline. **Trims multiply** because objects are pathed as separate islands. The rebuild therefore needs a geometry front end (primitive fitting, simplification at final size, splitting regions into structures) more than it needs better stitch generators, which Ink/Stitch and the expired Soft Sight patents already document in reusable detail. Many thresholds still come from single practitioners or SEO-grade blogs. The rules at the end are therefore marked sourced or inferred, and they belong in tunable fabric profiles calibrated by a sew-out.

## Professionals redraw at final size, and five vector objects become fifteen

The working method is the same across every serious source, and it begins before any stitch exists. Campbell's first rule is scale: a crane rendered for a hat or for a jacket back is "a very different set of discussions about what stitches are used and how much detail I can fit," because thread has a fixed thickness ([The Takeup Ep. 258, Erich Campbell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=1167s)). He then **measures each element at 100% of final size** before naming its stitch: "this thing is under this many millimeters wide, that can be a satin stitch" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=1310s)). Embroidery Legacy's cap demo scales 3.44 in artwork down to about 2.25 in before placing a single point, and it digitizes cap and flat versions separately ([How to Digitize Logos for Hats!, Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uLphQlj9tBo&t=63s)). Wilcom regenerates underlay whenever an object is scaled ([Wilcom automatic underlays](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-6.htm)). Embrilliance tells font makers to digitize at the intended size and then edit at the smallest size the design should sew ([Embrilliance font tips](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Win%201169/digitizingtipsforfontcreation.htm)). Line work is the least forgiving case. An engraving-style "orchards" piece scaled down 25% became "too tight… too dark… a mishmash", because drawn lines "are going to compress… they're not going to recalculate" the way fills do. Campbell's governing constraints are always "smallest stitch, smallest gap, longest stitch" ([The Takeup Ep. 272](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MiarfPVEExY&t=1962s)).

The second step decides what survives at that size, and the answer is driven by identity, not fidelity. For a mascot on a cap back, Campbell asked the client what mattered most and "isolated the most important aspect and gave it the detail it required". Otherwise the facial details "would have been about the size of an eraser on a pencil" ([The Takeup Ep. 21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T1OHsJxB66E&t=1007s)). For detailed logos with mottos and small text he advises "coordinate, don't replicate": build a cap-appropriate layout from elements of the logo instead of shrinking the whole thing ([Ep. 21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T1OHsJxB66E&t=1109s)). He sets text size by viewing distance, wants **at least about 5 mm** text on caps, and treats about **58 to 60 mm** as his comfortable cap height ([Ep. 21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T1OHsJxB66E&t=925s); [Ep. 21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T1OHsJxB66E&t=1211s)). Textures are the first thing to go. On a vehicle piece the client did not care about chrome reflections or background shading, so "some things from the original art get dropped out" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=921s)). Embroidery Legacy's checklist says the same in list form. Remove micro-details or enlarge them. Remove textures, shadows, gradients and busy backgrounds. Thicken hairline strokes and merge crowded micro-elements, because "smaller sizes need simpler shapes and wider spacing" ([Embroidery Legacy, bad artwork](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=199377)).

The third step is a redraw, not a conversion, and this is where the strongest practitioners part ways with auto-digitizing. "For me converting and editing existing vector shapes often takes longer than drawing it myself again," Campbell says. He builds compensation and layering in while redrawing, because print vectors lack the distortion that embroidery needs ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=3454s)). His "Prep and Punch" method plans everything together. Plan a stitching order the machine can run sensibly. Build layered objects that overlap. Draw shapes already compensated for pull and push. Find anything "too small to stitch, too close together, or need more overlap". Then give each shape its stitch type, angle and order as you draw it. Print-only construction such as hidden objects, masks and decorative borders is rebuilt or thrown away, and serifs are split from letter strokes ([Mr X Stitch, Prep and Punch](https://www.mrxstitch.com/prep-and-punch-drawing-and-embroidery-digitizing/)). The key to digitizing any image, he says, is to "break it up into smaller sections" even when the art shows one continuous colour, starting with the *structures* (body panels, tire sidewall versus tread, windscreen) ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=1082s)). In his worked example a **5-object source vector became 15 layered objects plus travel stitches** ([Mr X Stitch, Prep and Punch](https://mrxstitch.com/prep-and-punch-drawing-and-embroidery-digitizing/)). Even a single unbroken silhouette is carved into facets with different stitch angles so that light plays across it ([Pillars of Embroidery Digitizing, Campbell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AcEm-_NlpYM&t=1801s)).

Campbell's split between automation and judgment is the most useful framing for a rebuild. He likes automatic satin and underlay *generation*, but he dislikes automatic *interpretation*, which produces "lazy work… you take a big complex shape and you fill it from top to bottom in one angle" ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=3250s)). Wilcom's redwork demo shows the positive case. Clean, separated vector line art converts object by object to run, triple or backstitch with only "minor modifications" ([Wilcom Spotlight: Redwork Reimagined](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ieizIuXDkS0&t=463s)). Automation works when the input is already a set of embroidery objects. An auto-digitizer therefore has to perform the interpretation steps explicitly, as rules, before it reaches the generators it already has.

| Stage | What the professional does | What an auto-digitizer must compute |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Scales art to the final placement and measures at 100% | Convert everything to mm at final size; regenerate all stitches on resize |
| Survival | Keeps the identity-critical element; drops texture, shading, background | Rank elements by role; drop or simplify by role until size rules pass |
| Redraw | Places few deliberate straight and curve points, snaps lines straight | Fit primitives (lines, arcs) between detected corners; snap to axes |
| Structures | Splits colour areas into panels, ribbons and lines | Segment regions into structural parts before assigning stitch types |
| Classify | Names the stitch by measured width and role | Width bands plus column test plus role rules |
| Plan | Sets angle, overlap, compensation and order while drawing | Choose angles, overlaps and the layering graph before generating stitches |
| Path | One thread per colour; travel hidden under later stitching | Eulerian and route-inspection pathing with covered underpaths |
| Check | Views stitches alone at 1:1, slow-redraws, sews a test | Automated rubric, density heat map, test swatch |

## Width and role pick the stitch: run under 0.8 mm, satin to 10 mm, fill beyond

Campbell gives his bands in one breath. Running stitches are "great for all lines less than .8 mm in width". Satins suit elements "thinner than 10 mm but thicker than 0.8 mm". Most machines will not sew satins over about 12.4 mm without slowing, so "we probably don't want to be over 10 most of the time". Fill is "a good choice for elements wider than 10 mm", and wider strokes can use length-limited or auto-split satins ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=1884s); [Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=2563s)). The floor is the only real disagreement. Embroidery Legacy's logo demo says "stick to 1 millimeter minimum in thickness" and uses a two-pass run wherever an element is too small even for a 1 mm satin ([How to Digitize Logos, Embroidery Legacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=188s); [same, 33:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2P68985-Zak&t=2026s)). Jeff Fuller will not digitize satin columns narrower than 1 mm ([Impressions, Fuller](https://impressionsmagazine.com/?p=29138)). Melco says 1.2 mm ([Melco Small Lettering Guide](https://melco.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/article_attachments/200735289/Small_Lettering_Guide.doc)), and Embrilliance says a typical 1.5 mm ([Embrilliance font tips](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Win%201169/digitizingtipsforfontcreation.htm)). The ceiling for a plain satin on wearables sits lower than Campbell's 10 mm. Wilcom and Embroidery Legacy both auto-split at **7 mm**, "the maximum recommended for clothing", and warn that most machines stop and tie off at 10 to 12 mm, leaving a gap ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/mastering-basic-stitch-types-satin-stitch-and-tatami-stitch); [Embroidery Legacy fill guide](https://embroiderylegacy.com/?p=320606)). The reconciled band for 40 wt thread on garments is: **run under 0.8 mm; two-pass or bean run from 0.8 to 1.0 mm; satin from 1.0 mm (1.5 mm on pile and knits); plain satin to 7 mm; auto-split satin from 7 to 10 mm; fill above 10 mm.** Width alone still does not decide the stitch. Bernina's Wilcom-based auto-digitizer uses a satin line for constant-width details and, "if the width varies, the software applies satin fill automatically" ([Bernina help](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Automatic/automatic/automatic-7.htm)). The Soft Sight patent routes only "thin, predominantly regular" objects to satin ([US6947808B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6947808B2/en)).

Role matters as much as width, and the clearest worked example is a building. Campbell's "Village of Las Lunas" strike-off on a pique polo uses **satins** for the train tracks, the roof edge, the lintels and the door and window elements. **Fills** cover "the big flat areas of the roof and the walls" and the field. **Running stitches** handle small borders and details, with the white straight stitches around the windows deliberately offset. Manual straight stitches form the supports under the roof edge ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=4385s)). His general mapping is the same. "Big flat areas are often fills… cylindrical items and things that are small and ribbon-like… are often satin stitches. Very thin lines are often done with manual stitches… engraving-like shading [is] done with manual or running stitches" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=981s)). Fills never make good borders. They are the "coloring book stitch", and borders done in one-angle fill "look terrible… they don't turn into corners, they don't have smooth edges" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=2956s)). Hatch's own demo names the opposite auto-digitizer habit as a defect: it picks satin or tatami by area size, which gives an inconsistent look ([Auto-Digitizing in Hatch, Hatch Embroidery](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37qLWDVoVE&t=453s)). Narrow bars get their own rule. Campbell builds a satin crossbar at 1.00 to 1.20 mm. Below that he builds a bar about **0.80 mm** wide from **2 to 4 manually placed run passes** instead of a skinny satin ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)).

### Line art is sewn as two-pass tours, and spacing below 0.8 mm must be thinned

Engraving-style and redwork line art follows a strict pathing discipline. Everything is done "in tours": walk out a branch and back along the same line. "Everything is in two rows. So we have an even thickness on all of those lines… one connected filament of thread. There are no jumps or trims" ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=2758s)). Pass count is a routing tool. Odd counts, as in bean or triple runs, travel forward, while even counts return to the start ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=3167s)). Campbell explains why uniformity matters: one run pass is about **0.4 mm** thick and two are about **0.8 mm**, so any unplanned backtrack visibly bolds a line ([Mr X Stitch, Pathing](https://www.mrxstitch.com/pathing/)). Caroselli-Barnes applies the same limit to run lettering: "You shouldn't go over any part of the letter more than twice" ([Impressions, Caroselli-Barnes](https://impressionsmagazine.com/how-to-digitize-small-lettering/)). Tone in engraving work comes from "the closeness of the lines" in a single colour ([Ep. 149](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YNpT0NiGnas&t=515s)). Spacing therefore has hard limits. With 40 wt thread, lines **less than about 0.50 mm apart read as solid**, doubled lines need **0.80 mm or more** between them to stay separate, and dense engraving areas are opened up by skipping or deleting repeated detail lines ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). Run stitch length depends on curvature. A 3.5 mm run cuts across curves, and 1.5 to 2 mm follows the art ([Embroidery Legacy, straight and curved lines](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kqkOyKdkGNk&t=82s)). Wilcom uses about 2 mm for a roughly 5 in redwork design and 2.5 to 3 mm on a jacket back, and calls anything at 8 to 10 mm "too long" ([Redwork Reimagined](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ieizIuXDkS0&t=2253s)).

### Trees are rendered as a few textured masses, never leaf by leaf and never as a flat blob

No professional source sews foliage literally, and none sews it as one flat fill either. Campbell's small left-chest tree is built in layers. A half-density burgundy fill goes first, then a half-density orange fill offset with random penetrations. The branches and trunk are black runs and overlapping satins arranged "so it looks like a little bunched trunk". A manual fill is "worked in" on top with "wormy" stitches following the edges, so the leaves seem to sit at different depths. The fill settings shown are **single curve, 25% randomness and 4 mm stitch length** ([Ep. 149](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YNpT0NiGnas&t=1732s); [Ep. 149](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YNpT0NiGnas&t=2819s)). His autumn tree has "some filling behind it", running and manual stitches for the interior branches, and randomized manual highlight stitches: "we're intimating a detail. We're hinting at something" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=1718s)). A curved fill with randomized stitch length gives organic texture "without making individual feathers" ([Ep. 258](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k6ebSH3lvDA&t=4549s)). A digitizing service's tree guide adds simpler numbers: a few tatami clumps with slightly offset directions, Wilcom tatami random up to **80%**, a jagged edge of about **0.6 mm** for bushes, a stitch direction of **45 to 90°** for loft ("horizontal stitching looks flat"), and never every grass blade ([Ignition Drawing](https://blog.ignitiondrawing.com/digitizing-trees-leaves-grass-and-bushes/print/)). The stylized alternative is also documented: a tree logo built entirely from smoothly rotating satin segments, with care where many columns converge ([Threads tree logo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XSQzCySThtw&t=649s)). For a single-colour line-art tree in a logo, this research points to two acceptable outputs. One keeps the drawing as **two-pass run tours** wherever its lines can be thinned to at least 0.8 mm spacing. The other builds the canopy from **one to three textured masses** with random or curved fill, a jagged edge and run accents for the branches. A solid, single-angle, sharp-edged silhouette is the "blob" both approaches exist to avoid. The approach for a given tree, and when to switch, is an inference; the sources give only the recipes.

### Density hot spots, not detail itself, are what break thread

Detail fails when penetrations stack in a small area. Campbell names "3D density" as the cause of knots and breaks: too many needle drops stacked in a small spot, such as detail-packed areas **around 2 mm in diameter**, or cookie-cutter fill removal that leaves many tiny stitches under overlying stitching ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). Painterly competition pieces with too much detail in the eyes came out "sunken in", with bird-nesting from too many penetrations ([Ep. 272](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MiarfPVEExY&t=3221s)). Most "caps run poorly" complaints trace to intense density in a small space, or to tiny lettering on top of several layers of stitching ([Ep. 21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T1OHsJxB66E&t=4078s)). A single satin over a fill is rarely the problem; clusters of tight needle drops are ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). This makes a needle-drop heat map a better simplification trigger than raw feature counts.

### Serif lettering is rebuilt as satin columns, with serifs split off

Lettering rules are the best-agreed numbers in the field, and they cut directly against tracing a serif wordmark into fills. Fuller states: "I don't digitize satin text smaller than 5mm, or with satin columns narrower than 1mm." Small text almost always gets **centre-run underlay with a short stitch of about 1 mm** so it does not poke out, and fill letters get a **satin outline at least 1 mm wide**, sewn in one run with the fill so they stay registered ([Impressions, Fuller](https://impressionsmagazine.com/?p=29138)). Freeman says column width matters at least as much as the quarter-inch height rule. He bans edge-walk underlay on small letters, insists on some pull compensation, and avoids serifs and fancy fonts whose strokes vary in width ([ASI, Freeman](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/may-2018/keep-your-small-letters-crisp/)). Hatch rates its satin and serif fonts from **6 mm**, run-stitch block fonts at **4 to 6 mm** and scripts from **12 mm**, with lowercase at about 70% of cap height ([Hatch blog](https://hatchembroidery.com/resources/blog/choosing-the-right-size-embroidery-lettering-for-your-project)). A vendor video citing a Fine Line Digitizing guide says letters lose their shape below about 4 mm and that **fill lettering works best at 10 mm and up** ([Stitchist](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ElPS0vhszhY&t=61s)). Openings close below about **0.80 to 1.00 mm** with 40 wt thread. Campbell's fixes are specific: lower the A crossbar, widen the bowls of P and R, open e, a and g, and raise pull compensation, not the drawn width, when a satin sews thin ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). Caroselli-Barnes adds that small-letter bottoms should be a little more open than their tops, and that an O can drop slightly below the baseline so it pulls up into line ([Impressions, Caroselli-Barnes](https://impressionsmagazine.com/how-to-digitize-small-lettering/)).

Hand-digitized lettering is built from paired points across the stroke, a "ladder". Corners and curves use different points, and at a sharp corner the block ends and a new one starts. On curves the pairs sit closer together and perpendicular to the curve. Strokes overlap by **0.5 to 1.0 mm** at joins (the A's crossbar goes last), because auto-converted fonts put needle drops in awkward places and give "hairy" edges ([Hatch hand-lettering write-up](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/machine-embroidery-digitizing-tutorial-manual-lettering-with-digitize-blocks-in-hatch)). Embrilliance's font rules add that overlapping strokes keep stitch length equal to column width. Joining strokes should stay close to perpendicular, and "parallel to point" joins knot thread and break needles ([Embrilliance font tips](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Win%201169/digitizingtipsforfontcreation.htm)). In a Wilcom logo walkthrough, the letter O was knifed diagonally into two gentler curves to stop satin bunching ([OKC logo write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/digitizing-the-okc-logo-in-wilcom-2025-tracing-angles-sequence-settings-with-push-pull-that-actually-prevents-gaps)). The Soft Sight patent even encodes serif detection: a degree-3 skeleton node with two short branches ending in degree-1 nodes is a serif ([US6947808B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6947808B2/en)). For a large serif "TENNESSEE", the implication is direct. Stems, bowls and arms under about 7 to 10 mm wide become satin columns. Serifs become separate short satins tucked under or over the stem. Fill is used only where a stem exceeds the satin ceiling, and then with edge-run underlay and a satin or run border.

## Settings have ceilings: 0.40 mm density, 3 to 4 mm underlay, a fifth of a millimetre of compensation

Density is capped, not tuned upward. Campbell says top-stitch density with 40 wt thread should rarely be tighter than **0.40 mm (4-point) spacing**, and "even in the blended areas, the design never goes tighter than .4mm spacing" ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/); [ErichCampbell.com](https://www.erichcampbell.com/commercial-embroidery-digitizing-technical-considerations/)). In his framing, 0.30 mm is "about 25% more than full coverage" ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=490s)), and a practitioner guide lists spacing tighter than 0.30 mm as a thread-break cause ([satin rules guide](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/satin-stitch-digitizing-that-actually-sews-clean-point-counterpoint-1-8-mm-rules-and-settings-that-stop-pinching)). A service bureau's fabric table runs **cotton twill 0.40 to 0.45 mm, poly twill 0.35 to 0.40, fleece 0.30 to 0.35 with topping, and knit jersey 0.45 to 0.50** ([Infinite Digitizing](https://www.infinitedigitizing.com/blog/embroidery-digitizing-quality/)). A fill sewn on top of another fill should be about a third lighter ([Stitchist](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ElPS0vhszhY&t=61s)). Satin spacing is the open conflict. Wilcom's Auto Spacing makes a 4 mm column denser (0.40 mm becomes **0.36 mm** at 90%) ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/stitch-spacing-or-density)). Freeman's "correct" top density in one example is 3.6 points ([ASI, Freeman](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/february-2018/common-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/)). That is tighter than Campbell's ceiling, so wide satins at 0.36 to 0.38 mm remain defensible, but nothing on 40 wt should go below about 0.35 mm.

### Density numbers need calibration before they are trusted

A unit trap hides under all of these figures. Wilcom states that tatami spacing is **measured across every second row**, because rows alternate between a stitch line and a back stitch ([Wilcom blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/stitch-spacing-or-density)). Industry stitch-count estimators put solid fill at about **2,000 stitches per square inch** ([CH Holderby](https://www.chholderby.com/tips-tricks-and-help/estimating-stitches/)). Jimmy Lamb counts a 10 × 40 mm shape at 4-point density as **626 stitches in fill versus 217 in satin** ([ASI, Lamb](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/october-2019/how-to-reduce-stitch-count-without-jeopardizing-quality/)). A naive single-pass scan-line fill at 0.40 mm rows and 4 mm stitches puts only about 250 stitches in that shape and roughly 400 per square inch. The satin figure matches 0.40 mm geometry closely, so the fill gap is not a measurement artefact. The likely explanation is that commercial "0.40 mm" fills lay more thread than a literal 0.40 mm row grid. If so, Ink/Stitch's 0.25 mm default row spacing is closer to commercial practice than it looks, and an earlier conclusion that it is "far denser than commercial practice" deserves a caveat. This is an inference from three numbers that do not fully reconcile. The safe engineering response is to measure the generator's stitches per square inch against these benchmarks and a sew-out before settling on a spacing value. Patchy coverage on fills is the symptom to watch.

### Underlay carries coverage and edge quality

When fabric shows through, Campbell adds structural underlay rather than density. He uses tatami underlay **perpendicular to the top stitching, at 3.00 to 4.00 mm spacing and 3.00 to 4.00 mm stitch length, inset at least 0.40 mm** from the edge, or a double mesh at 45° and 135° from the object's angle ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). That is far more open than Ink/Stitch's default of three times the top row spacing ([Ink/Stitch fill_stitch.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/elements/fill_stitch.py)). He also warns never to delete edge-run underlay, because it supports edges and **prevents sawtoothing**. "Escaped underlay", a stray row that looks like a misplaced outline, is cured by raising pull compensation if the satin sews thin, or else by insetting the underlay at least 0.40 mm, not by deleting it ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). Wilcom reserves centre-run underlay for narrow columns, "e.g. 2-3 mm wide", and pairs edge run with zigzag on wider shapes ([Wilcom run underlay](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-10.htm)). StitchArtist sets edge run in about half a needle width, which also eases tight corners ([StitchArtist underlay](https://embrilliance.com/Help/Platform%20Win%201165/underlay.htm)). Freeman's typo case fixes a sane walk-underlay stitch length at **2 mm (20 points)**. A mistaken 0.2 mm nearly doubled a border's stitch count, from 772 to 1,297, and broke thread ([ASI, Freeman](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/february-2018/common-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/)). Small letters are contested. Fuller wants a 1 mm centre run. Caroselli-Barnes and a Ricoma cap tutorial remove underlay on tiny letters ([Impressions, Caroselli-Barnes](https://impressionsmagazine.com/how-to-digitize-small-lettering/); [Ricoma small letters on caps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P4nluzCf-xs&t=189s)). Every source forbids edge walk on them.

### Compensation is directional, and its size is now sourced

Campbell gives the first practitioner numbers for both distortions. Pull narrows satin columns by about **0.17 to 0.25 mm** on stable materials, and fills less. Push lengthens full-density 40 wt satins at their ends by about **0.13 to 0.20 mm**. Pull is fixed by drawing wider along the stitch angle. Push in fills acts perpendicular to the fill angle, so the shape is drawn slightly shorter in that direction, and a satin or fill should stop **at least 0.17 to 0.20 mm** short of a border in the push direction ([Impressions, Campbell, push and pull](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/isolating-embroidery-problems-digitizing-vs-execution-pull-push-distortion/168424/)). Demonstrated settings sit in the same range. A PE-Design tutorial uses **0.3 mm** for cotton shirts and polos, **0.4 mm or more** for performance knits and **0.1 to 0.2 mm** for denim or canvas, applied to the pull sides only ([PE-Design 10 write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/pe-design-10-digitizing-fundamentals-the-only-three-stitch-types-and-how-pull-compensation-saves-your-registration)). Wilcom's own table is drill 0.20, T-shirt 0.35, fleece 0.40 and lettering 0.2 to 0.3 mm on both sides ([Wilcom pull compensation](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-17.htm)). One Wilcom logo walkthrough used **0.25 to 0.35 mm** on satin borders and **0.70 mm** on a white background fill ([OKC logo write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/digitizing-the-okc-logo-in-wilcom-2025-tracing-angles-sequence-settings-with-push-pull-that-actually-prevents-gaps)). Wilcom notes that experienced digitizers often compensate fills by overlapping objects manually instead of using automatic "overstitching" ([Wilcom docs](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/stabilizing/stabilizing-16.htm)). Campbell's calibration rule closes the loop. Sew a sample and measure the gap between outline and fill; that gap is the minimum compensation. Unstable materials can need border overlap approaching 100% of the border width ([Mr X Stitch, pull compensation](https://www.mrxstitch.com/pull-compensation/)). If a sew-out shows a 1 mm gap, extend the fill 1 mm or more under the border ([The Takeup 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKDMAc3yyNs&t=1245s)).

### Fill angles avoid 45°, avoid perpendicular borders and differ between neighbours

The angle rule in this research corrects common practice. Campbell explains that a satin border running perpendicular to a fill "grab[s] the top of the fill" and opens gaps. Most people rotate the fill to 45°, but "45 degrees is a weak angle on your fabric… back it down to like 15 to 20 degrees… 20 degrees off the horizontal is enough that when it tries to grab that fill it will not break up in the edges" ([Pillars, Campbell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AcEm-_NlpYM&t=2376s)). In print he adds that vertical fills should be avoided on knits and caps ([Impressions, Campbell, push and pull](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/isolating-embroidery-problems-digitizing-vs-execution-pull-push-distortion/168424/)). This conflicts with a PE-Design tutorial that defaults fills to 45° to keep out of the "weave ditch" ([PE-Design 10 write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/pe-design-10-digitizing-fundamentals-the-only-three-stitch-types-and-how-pull-compensation-saves-your-registration)). Campbell's reasoning about bias stretch is the stronger source. Layering in the same angle as the main fill causes cupping, the "taco" or "potato chip" effect ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/)). A satin running the same direction as the fill beneath it sinks in, so a fill under text sits at a contrasting angle: one demo uses a 45° fill under 90° text ([Embroidery Legacy run/satin/fill write-up](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/run-satin-and-fill-the-only-3-stitch-types-you-need-to-digitize-and-stitch-almost-anything)). Neighbours differ on purpose. A Wilcom demo puts 90° beside 135°, a left-chest tutorial reverses the middle of three houses, and Embird warns that adjacent fills at the same angle interleave into a jagged edge ([OKC logo write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/digitizing-the-okc-logo-in-wilcom-2025-tracing-angles-sequence-settings-with-push-pull-that-actually-prevents-gaps); [Embroidery Training Videos](https://www.embroiderytrainingvideos.com/tutorials/digitize-simple-left-chest-logo-from-scratch/); [Embird tutorial](https://www.embird.net/machine-embroidery-tutorials/studio-next/hw_digitize_2.htm)). Campbell describes seams and edges "by changing stitch angles" ([The Takeup Ep. 145](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rbk1uZCG2WU&t=3842s)). Inside one object, the Soft Sight patent's search of **16 scan angles for the fewest fragments** remains the best automatic tie-breaker ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en)). When two abutting fills do share an angle, Ink/Stitch aligns their rows and stagger to a global grid so no seam shows ([Ink/Stitch fill.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/fill.py)).

### Overlaps are measured in tenths of a millimetre, and knockouts are for big shapes only

Overlap figures cluster by joint type. A satin border overlaps its fill by **0.3 to 0.5 mm**, usually supplied by pull compensation ([Wilcom basics write-up](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/wilcom-embroiderystudio-digitizing-basics-clean-text-smart-shapes-and-overlap-free-logos-that-actually-stitch-well)). Strokes overlap **0.5 to 1.0 mm** at letter joins ([Hatch hand-lettering write-up](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/machine-embroidery-digitizing-tutorial-manual-lettering-with-digitize-blocks-in-hatch)). A vendor tutorial runs fills under satin borders by **30 to 40% of the border width** ([Embroidery Hoop Store](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/stop-the-gaps-dialing-in-push-pull-compensation-in-pe-design-so-your-fills-and-satins-actually-meet-the-outline)), against Campbell's "up to 100%" on unstable goods. Too much overlap makes lumps and adds stitches ([Echidna Sewing](https://www.echidnasewing.com.au/article/embroidery/understanding-compensation/)). Knockouts follow the size of the cutter. A Hatch lesson cuts holes in the layer below only for top objects larger than about **4 mm**, because stacked fill-fill-satin layers overload the needle ([Hatch lesson write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/stop-fighting-auto-digitize-clean-manual-artwork-digitizing-in-hatch-embroidery-2-without-bulky-bulletproof-stitches)). Campbell leaves "spaces made in underlying layers for top stitching wherever possible" ([ErichCampbell.com](https://www.erichcampbell.com/commercial-embroidery-digitizing-technical-considerations/)), but he blames cookie-cutter remnants of tiny stitches for breaks. Auto-digitizers get this backwards in a telling way. Hatch's demo shows the auto result leaving **a hole under an object that sews on top**, because "it can't see the pink running under here". A human "would just fill that in", since push and pull open gaps ([Auto-Digitizing in Hatch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37qLWDVoVE&t=453s)). Wilcom's Remove Overlaps keeps a cutting overlap, excludes narrow cutters and drops tiny fragments, but it publishes no defaults ([Wilcom remove overlaps](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/quality/quality-2.htm)). The workable synthesis is to keep the lower layer continuous under small and narrow top objects, and to cut it under large top fills while leaving 0.5 to 1 mm of overlap.

## One thread per colour: sequencing, hidden travel and trims

The professional order is back to front and centre out, with registration overriding efficiency. Campbell's "tablecloth" rule starts in the rough centre and pushes outward, never stitching toward areas already secured to the stabilizer, which traps a wave of fabric. He uses each colour as few times as possible, but stays within one area when changing colours so registration does not drift. In his sample design **every colour is one continuous thread** except a final white accent ([Mr X Stitch, Pathing](https://www.mrxstitch.com/pathing/)). On large designs he splits colour stops so that one area finishes all its colours before the machine moves on. His rule: "Only jump, trim, or change colors when maintaining registration or properly layering elements requires it" ([Impressions, Campbell 2026](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/); [ErichCampbell.com](https://www.erichcampbell.com/commercial-embroidery-digitizing-technical-considerations/)). A Wilcom logo walkthrough orders a whole-logo run underlay first, then backgrounds, then text, then borders, grouped by colour ([OKC logo write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/digitizing-the-okc-logo-in-wilcom-2025-tracing-angles-sequence-settings-with-push-pull-that-actually-prevents-gaps)). Hatch's guidance is "from the background to the foreground, from the center out and from the largest areas to the smallest" ([Auto-Digitizing in Hatch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37qLWDVoVE&t=200s)). Caps run bottom-up and centre-out. On small cap designs Freeman puts the border last so it shares a colour stop with the element before it ([ASI, Freeman](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/february-2018/common-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/)). Automatic colour sorting is acceptable only if it respects layering. Wilcom's Optimize Color Changes keeps all existing overlaps ([Wilcom auto-sequence](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Modifying/combine/combine-16.htm)), and Ink/Stitch notes that a colour must be split when it lies both under and over another ([Ink/Stitch routing](https://inkstitch.org/tutorials/routing/)).

Travel is hidden, and it hugs the middle of whatever covers it. Campbell lists "travel stitches escaping their top stitching" among common distortion problems ([Impressions, Campbell, push and pull](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/isolating-embroidery-problems-digitizing-vs-execution-pull-push-distortion/168424/)). Ink/Stitch's fill underpath encodes the cure. Travel runs on an interior lattice at the fill angle ±45° (2 mm spacing) plus −90° (√2 mm), with spacing halved for small shapes. Interior edges are weighted by **length ÷ (distance to outline + 0.1)** and boundary edges by **3 × length**. Lattice edges are deleted once a row crossing them is sewn, so travel never runs on top of finished stitching ([Ink/Stitch tatami_fill.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/tatami_fill.py)). Wilcom's trim-reduction workflow is the commercial counterpart. Find the trims, use Branching to join touching objects into one object with a single entry and exit, apply Closest Join to align exits with entries, and resequence into a continuous flow, with "Inside" connectors between letters ([Wilcom minimizing trims](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/minimizing-thread-trims-in-embroidery); [Wilcom branching](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/connectors/connectors-20.htm)). Closest Join is not maintained after objects are moved or edited, so long connectors creep in unless it is re-applied ([Wilcom minimizing connectors](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/27/en/OnlineHelp/Quality/connectors/connectors-16.htm)). An automatic pipeline should recompute entries and exits after every structural change.

### The open algorithms already solve one-pass pathing

Ink/Stitch is the only inspectable implementation, and its algorithms map directly onto the trim problem. Note that `auto_fill.py` is now `lib/stitches/tatami_fill.py`, and that the satin column is now a package ([Ink/Stitch file listing](https://api.github.com/repos/inkstitch/inkstitch/contents/lib/stitches)). Fill is a graph problem. Each scan-line run becomes an edge, and the boundary between run endpoints is cut into outline edges, **every other one doubled** so an Eulerian path exists (`networkx.eulerize` is the fallback). A Hierholzer-style walk that **prefers row edges** then sews the whole region, holes included, in one pass ([tatami_fill.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/tatami_fill.py)). Where travel runs along the outline after more than three rows, `fill_gaps` re-sews extra rows to cover it. The legacy fill joins runs on adjacent rows only if they are within 1.1 × spacing and the quadrilateral between them is **at least 90% inside** the shape, which stops joins across the gap between an H's legs ([fill.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/fill.py)). Between objects, `add_jumps` connects separate components with the minimum total length of jumps (`k_edge_augmentation`, effectively a minimum spanning tree). `find_path` then walks a shortest start-to-end path and explores branches depth-first, re-sewing edges on the way back as underpaths ([Ink/Stitch autoroute.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/utils/autoroute.py)). Auto-satin dices satins into 1 mm pieces so jumps can land anywhere. A section that appears twice is sewn as running stitch the first time and satin the last. It trims only when a jump's **uncovered length exceeds 1 mm** ([auto_satin.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inkstitch/inkstitch/main/lib/stitches/auto_satin.py)), and auto-run trims jumps over **0.75 mm** ([auto_run.py](https://git.push-f.com/inkstitch/plain/lib/stitches/auto_run.py?h=nix)). For line networks, Ink/Stitch prefers "Redwork": exactly two passes per line, ending where it started ([Ink/Stitch routing](https://inkstitch.org/tutorials/routing/)). The Chinese-postman alternative, which doubles only the cheapest edges, is shorter but gives uneven line weight. Campbell's two-pass rule says uniform weight wins. The Soft Sight patent covers the satin side. It traverses the skeleton graph recursively aiming for **zero thread cuts**, and where an interruption is unavoidable it picks the option that minimises the length of columns sewn during it ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en)).

### Trims cost about a hundred stitches each, and the target is colour blocks minus one

No authoritative trims-per-1,000-stitches benchmark exists. The best metric is Campbell's: one thread per colour block, so the minimum trims are about the number of colour blocks minus one, plus unavoidable islands. Trim cost estimates range from **about 70 stitches** of run time per trim ([Wilcom Help](https://help.wilcom.com/portal/en/kb/articles/embroidery-fonts-printing-fonts)) to **about 120**. At 700 stitches per minute, 25 trims add about four minutes per piece, and each leaves a scratchy knot on the back ([Embroidery Legacy pathing](https://embroiderylegacy.com/pathing-embroidery-design-digitizing/)). Hatch's demo shows the auto-digitizer baseline: **44,523 stitches, 10 colours and 55 trims** from instant auto-digitizing, with trims halved simply by adding connector and travel runs ([Auto-Digitizing in Hatch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37qLWDVoVE&t=110s); [same, 9:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37qLWDVoVE&t=545s)). A Wilcom logo walkthrough's go/no-go list asks for **fewer than 6 trims** on a left-chest logo and no stitch under 0.3 mm ([OKC logo write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/digitizing-the-okc-logo-in-wilcom-2025-tracing-angles-sequence-settings-with-push-pull-that-actually-prevents-gaps)). That figure comes from an SEO-grade summary, so it is a soft target. Trim thresholds cluster at 1 to 2 mm of uncovered connector: Ink/Stitch uses 1 mm (0.75 mm for runs), and BERNINA's Wilcom-based software defaults to **2 mm** ([BERNINA connectors](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Digitizing/connectors/connectors-4.htm)). The file must carry trims the machine will honour. Brother PR machines turn a configured number of consecutive DST jumps (for example 3) into a trim, and that count must match the file ([Brother PR-620 manual](https://www.manualowl.com/m/Brother%20International/PR-620/Manual/255808?page=166)). DST caps any single stitch or jump at **121 units (12.1 mm)**, so longer moves are split ([EduTech Wiki DST](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Embroidery_format_DST)). Ties belong only around trims and colour changes, and tie-off distance should be no longer than trim distance ([Wilcom connectors blog](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/connectors-the-functions-between-objects)).

## Auto-digitizers fail by tracing colour blocks, and each failure has a known fix

Commercial documentation describes a shared recipe, not algorithms. Wilcom's instant auto-digitize "attempt[s] to classify image colors as fills or details". The user can set each colour to fill, detail or omit. Details become branched Satin, Column C or Double Run objects, and "fills are stitched first, details last" with closest join ([Wilcom auto-digitize](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/26/en/OnlineHelp/Automatic/automatic/Auto-digitize_entire_designs.htm)). Bernina routes constant-width details to satin line and variable-width ones to satin fill ([Bernina help](https://softwarehelp.bernina.com/en/MainHelp/PART_Automatic/automatic/automatic-7.htm)). Wilcom's Auto Fabric then adjusts density, pull, coverage and underlay per fabric without publishing numbers; caps get tighter density, strong edge underlay and more tie-offs ([Wilcom auto fabrics](https://docs.wilcom.com/embroiderystudio/28/en/OnlineHelp/Digitizing/properties/properties-9.htm)). Brother's Auto Punch exposes only colour count, noise reduction and segmentation sensitivity, and it separates regions from lines ([PE-DESIGN 11 Auto Punch](http://support.brother.com/g/s/hf/htmldoc/ped/im/ped11/en/PED11_EN/pages/06_1807865.html)). The expired Soft Sight patents (priority 1998) remain the most complete public pipeline. Their steps run as follows. Start from 300 dpi input. Apply edge-preserving smoothing and region growing, and merge objects under 6 pixels. Compute chain codes, a (3,4) chamfer distance transform and a skeleton. Classify thick objects as fill and thin, regular ones as satin, using the maximum, mean and standard deviation of distance values on the skeleton. Simplify contours by "triangular filtering", with **fixed vertices where neighbouring objects touch** so shared borders stay identical. Pick each fill's angle as the one of 16 with the fewest fragments, and order fragments recursively to minimise cuts, with running stitches along each fragment's mid-line. Build satins from least-squares "stroke normals", anchor column ends to sharp contour tips (under about 30°), and split at bends sharper than about 45° ([US6836695B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6836695B1/en); [US6947808B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6947808B2/en); [US9200397](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9200397)). Ink/Stitch, by contrast, does not classify at all. The user draws the objects, and that is precisely the interpretation step a rebuild must supply. The strongest recent academic work, Liu and colleagues' "Directionality-Aware Design of Embroidery Patterns" (Eurographics 2023), seeds stitch streamlines at the sources and sinks of a direction field. It notes that colour density and direction cannot both be satisfied exactly ([CGF 2023](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf14770); [project page](https://misop.github.io/projects/Embroidery/index.html)). That is a future route to curved fills that follow a shape, beyond the guided fill Ink/Stitch already offers.

Practitioner critiques of auto output are remarkably consistent. "The computer can see edges of blocks of color… it looks for the edges of colored blocks" ([Sewing Report, downsides of auto digitizing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjGcd7flG-Y&t=0s)). Its diver logo came out "all fill stitch going in two directions", with "too many stitch penetration points; this is going to break thread". Its griffin had big split satins, detail "paved over" and no compensation ([Sewing Report](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjGcd7flG-Y&t=126s); [same](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjGcd7flG-Y&t=250s)). Blurry art yields satin edges traced along pixel "stairs" of about **0.2 mm**, needle hammering and circles of **about 500 nodes** ([Hatch zoom test](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/stop-auto-digitizing-blurry-art-in-hatch-the-zoom-test-that-saves-hours-and-thread)). A hobbyist channel names node count and over-zoomed digitizing as top mistakes: "when in doubt reduce that node count" ([easydigitize](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3agiYLeRypY&t=65s)). Professionals evaluate smoothness at 1:1. Wobbles visible at 600 to 2,100% zoom are irrelevant, because "reality in embroidery is one-to-one" ([Embroidery Legacy, straight and curved lines](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kqkOyKdkGNk&t=288s)). They draw with deliberate straight and curve points ("straight, straight… curve, curve…"), holding Shift for "a perfectly straight line" and exact 0/180° angles ([How to Digitize Logos for Hats!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uLphQlj9tBo&t=294s); [same](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uLphQlj9tBo&t=501s)). A Hatch lesson needs only **3 to 4 nodes for a circle** ([Hatch lesson write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/stop-fighting-auto-digitize-clean-manual-artwork-digitizing-in-hatch-embroidery-2-without-bulky-bulletproof-stitches)). Mapped onto the four symptoms in the current output, the sources give a direct diagnosis.

| Symptom in current output | Root cause the sources identify | What professionals do | Codeable fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight walls sew as wobbly traced lines | Contours traced from pixel stairs (~0.2 mm steps), hundreds of nodes, no corner/straight distinction | Redraw with few straight points, snap to exact angles, judge at 1:1 | Corner detection, then least-squares line fitting between corners; axis snapping; minimal nodes; needle points on vertices |
| Trees sew as blobs | Line art merged into one colour block and flood-filled at one angle | Few textured masses (random, half-density, jagged edge) plus run accents, or two-pass run tours | Thin line clusters to at least 0.8 mm spacing and keep them as runs; otherwise textured partial-density fill with jagged edge |
| Serif fill letters have ragged, patchy edges | Tatami on narrow serifed strokes, no edge underlay or outline, no compensation, hairlines under 1 mm | Satin ladder columns, serifs split off, overlap joins 0.5 to 1 mm, satin outline at least 1 mm on fill letters | Column test per stroke; serif detector; satin under 7 to 10 mm; fill only above, with edge run, comp and border |
| Too many trims | Objects pathed as islands; no covered travel; closest join not recomputed | One thread per colour, travel under later stitching, branching | Layering graph, colour grouping, Eulerian fills, MST jumps plus DFS underpaths, trims only when uncovered travel exceeds 1 to 2 mm |

## A usable file passes measurable checks and one sew-out

Practitioners judge a file by what happens at the machine. Thread breaks in the same spot implicate the file, not the machine ([The Standard Digitizing](https://www.thestandarddigitizing.com/2026/06/embroidery-thread-breaks-same-spot.html)). Freeman's warning signs are repeated breaks, puckering, holes in the fabric and letter openings filling in ([ASI, Freeman](https://members.asicentral.com/news/how-to/may-2018/keep-your-small-letters-crisp/)). On the back of a good satin, about a third of the column width should show bobbin thread ([satin rules guide](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/satin-stitch-digitizing-that-actually-sews-clean-point-counterpoint-1-8-mm-rules-and-settings-that-stop-pinching)). Before sewing, professionals hide the artwork to judge the stitches alone, slow-redraw to check entries, exits and travel, and run the software's object checker. Ink/Stitch's Troubleshoot Objects flags errors in red and warnings in yellow, and it can generate test swatches from a selection ([Hatch zoom test](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/stop-auto-digitizing-blurry-art-in-hatch-the-zoom-test-that-saves-hours-and-thread); [Wilcom basics write-up](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/wilcom-embroiderystudio-digitizing-basics-clean-text-smart-shapes-and-overlap-free-logos-that-actually-stitch-well); [Ink/Stitch troubleshooting](https://inkstitch.org/tutorials/troubleshoot/)). Every expert ends with a test sew-out on the real fabric and stabilizer, changing one variable at a time ([Mr X Stitch, 3 digitizing truths](https://www.mrxstitch.com/3-digitizing-truths/)). Simulators such as Hatch's TrueView do not model fabric stretch or tension ([Hatch lesson write-up](https://embroideryhoopstore.com/blogs/articles/stop-fighting-auto-digitize-clean-manual-artwork-digitizing-in-hatch-embroidery-2-without-bulky-bulletproof-stitches)). The checks below turn this practice into a rubric. The thresholds are sourced, but combining them into pass, warn and fail bands is an engineering choice.

| Check (40 wt thread, woven or stabilized) | Pass | Warn | Fail | Source of threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top row spacing | 0.38 to 0.50 mm | 0.30 to 0.38 or 0.50 to 0.60 mm | under 0.30 mm | [Campbell](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/); [satin rules](https://embroideryhooping.com/blogs/articles/satin-stitch-digitizing-that-actually-sews-clean-point-counterpoint-1-8-mm-rules-and-settings-that-stop-pinching) |
| Penetration hot spot (any 2 mm circle, all layers) | underlay + 1 top layer | 2 top layers | 3 or more, or tiny remnant stitches | [Campbell](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-part-2preventing-embroidery-digitizing-disasters-lettering-density-underlay-problems/169753/) |

## Conclusion

The research shifts where the rebuild's effort belongs. The earlier framing treated stitch generation as mostly solved and classification as the lever. This research puts the decisive lever one step earlier still, in **geometry reconstruction**. Professionals never let a stitch generator see a traced contour. They feed it a handful of deliberate primitives, snapped and split into structures, and they treat line art as a routing problem (two-pass tours at 0.8 mm minimum spacing) rather than a region-filling problem. Two corrections matter in practice. Fill angles should sit 15 to 20° off horizontal rather than at the popular 45°. And coverage should come from 3 to 4 mm perpendicular underlay rather than tighter density. Both are cheap to adopt and both target the "patchy" look directly.

The biggest remaining uncertainty is density calibration. The stitch-count benchmarks and Wilcom's every-second-row convention suggest that commercial "0.40 mm" fills lay more thread than a literal 0.40 mm scan grid. This should be settled with the generator's own stitches-per-area figures and one sew-out before any spacing default is frozen. Beyond that, the sources stop at recipes. No one publishes when a tree should switch from runs to textured fill, how much axis snapping is safe, or how many trims a cap logo should carry. Those numbers have to come from controlled sew-outs of the test logo, which the rules below are designed to make cheap.

## Prioritized rules for an auto-digitizer

How to read this section: **Sourced** means both the number and the logic come from the cited sources. **Mixed** means the numbers are sourced but the combination or the if/then structure is an engineering choice. **Inferred** means a reasoned proposal with no direct source, whose numbers are starting points to tune. Defaults assume 40 wt thread on wearables, with caps counted as wearables, measured in mm at the final sewn size. The "Change" column relates each rule to the earlier 42-rule list ("old n"). The priorities follow the four symptoms in the current output: geometry first, then simplification, classification, lettering, settings, satin construction, layering, pathing and checks.

### Addendum (November 2026): lessons from the Embroidery Legacy digitizing group

The rules below come from the Embroidery Legacy group's guides and from members' advice on real
stitch-outs (a private Facebook group of the software's users). They are summarized in our own
words, and the digitizer now follows each one.

| # | Rule | Basis | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 61 | **Travel on the edge in light fills.** In normal-density fills, travel through the middle is hidden. In light or half-density fills (foliage, textures) it shows, so travel keeps to the shape's edge. | Embroidery Legacy guide 11, tip 52 "Travel On Edge" | New: textured fills route travel along the edge |
| 62 | **Split wide satin instead of filling it.** A satin wider than the longest safe stitch (7 mm) is split across the column with staggered needle points, so it keeps the satin look without loose stitches. Only strokes over about twice that width become fill. | Tip 99 "Middle Split"; Wilcom auto split | New: every satin over 7 mm is split; lettering stays satin up to 14 mm strokes |
| 63 | **Narrow satin, not bean, for 4 to 6 mm lettering.** Bean letters sink into twill, pique and cap fabric and look pale from 3 ft. A satin 0.8 to 1.2 mm wide (about a fifth of the cap height) reads bolder and still leaves the counters open. Under 4 mm, letters stay running stitch. | Members' advice on a left-chest logo with bean lettering on duck/denim ("1 mm steil… maybe 0.5 mm") | Revised (rule 25): narrow satin from 4 mm |
| 64 | **Equal passes on every stroke.** A letter's strokes must all get the same number of passes, or the strokes with fewer passes look lighter. | Same thread (the F's bar and the H's right side looked pale) | Confirmed: run letters sew every stroke out and back as a bean (4 passes) |
| 65 | **Shorter running stitches on tiny shapes.** Long stitches turn small curves into corners. Tiny letters use stitches of about 0.4 × the cap height (0.8 to 1.25 mm). | Tip 48 "Shorten running stitch length to smooth smaller objects" | New |
